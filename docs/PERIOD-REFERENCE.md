# PERIOD REFERENCE — Street Stickball, New York City, September 1925

**What this is.** A modeller's and writer's dossier for one block of a New York City side
street in the autumn of 1925, and for the game of stickball played on it. Every entry is meant
to be *buildable*: a dimension, a count, a color, a material, a word somebody actually said.
Where a fact is period-documented it is stated flat. Where it is a defensible reconstruction it
is marked `~`. Where a thing is a common movie lie it is marked `✗` and lives in §9.

**How to use it.**
- Builders: §2–§4 are geometry and texture. §5–§7 are behavior, dialogue, and audio. Numbers
  in feet; metric in parentheses where it matters. Hex values are art direction, not archival
  paint chips — they are tuned to read correctly under the §6 lighting, not to match a swatch.
- Critics: §8 is the checklist — **40 items**. A frame scoring below **25 of 40**, or containing
  anything from §9, is not 1925 New York; it is "old timey city," and it fails.

**Verification pass, 2026-08-18.** Every dated claim below was re-checked against sources. Items
that failed are corrected in place and the failure is named, because a corrected fact you can't
see the shape of is a fact that grows back. The three that mattered most: the **pink Spalding
ball is a 1930s object** and has been demoted out of the certain column (§1.2); a **cross-street
blockface is 600–920 ft, not 200 ft** (§2.1); and two of the **movie posters were for films that
had not opened yet** (§4.5).

**The premise, fixed.** Late afternoon, **Tuesday, 22 September 1925** — the day before that
year's autumnal equinox. Pin the date; every light number in §6 is computed from it.

**Clocks.** New York was on **daylight saving time** that afternoon. Federal DST was repealed in
1919, but the city kept its own, and in 1925 it ran **26 April – 27 September**. So the scene is
five days from the changeover and every time in this document is **EDT**. Computing the sun on
standard time puts every shadow an hour wrong, and an hour at 4 pm in September is 11° of
altitude — the difference between a lit street and a dark one.

One **mid-block stretch, ~200 ft / 8 lots**, of an east–west cross street in a walk-up tenement
neighborhood — read it as East Harlem / the South Bronx / the Lower East Side; the block type is
the same in all three. This is a *stretch* of a blockface, not the whole thing (§2.1). The El is
a block and a half away and audible. School let out at three. Nobody's father is home yet. The
game has been going for forty minutes and there has been one argument.

**Alternate date, if you want the block dressed for an election:** **Tuesday, 15 September
1925**, the day of the Democratic mayoral primary, when State Senator **Jimmy Walker** beat the
sitting mayor **John F. Hylan**, 248,338 to 154,204. Identical weather, sun altitude within
2°, plus poll-watchers, a school in use as a polling place, and fresh bills pasted over old ones.

**What is in the papers that week.** Free, checkable, and it gives every adult in frame
something to be arguing about instead of standing there being scenery:

- **The anthracite strike, three weeks old.** ~150,000 hard-coal miners struck on **1 September
  1925** and stayed out 176 days, into February 1926. Hard coal is short and dear at the very
  start of the heating season — which is *why* this block is burning smoky soft bituminous coal
  (§6.3). The haze in §6.3 is not atmosphere-for-mood; it is the news.
- **Walker beat Hylan** on 15 September. The general election against the Republican **Frank D.
  Waterman** (of the fountain pens) is 3 November.
- **Babe Ruth's ruined season.** Fined **$5,000** and suspended by manager Miller Huggins in late
  August — the largest fine ever levied on a player at the time — reinstated and hitting again
  through September. The Yankees finish **69–85, seventh, 30 games out**. The **Senators** run
  away with the American League; the **Pirates** clinch the National **tomorrow, 23 September**,
  and will take the World Series in seven — so today the pennant is still, just barely, an
  argument. A stoop full of men in shirtsleeves has all of this to be wrong about.

**Unit convention.** 1 world unit = 1 foot. A five-story tenement is 60 units tall. The street
is 60 units wide, facade to facade. If the engine uses meters, multiply by 0.3048 and keep the
foot numbers in comments, because every number below is a foot number and rounding them in
meters is how blocks stop looking like blocks.

# 1. THE GAME

## 1.1 The bat

- A **broom handle or mop handle**, stolen or salvaged. Not a bat. Never a bat.
- Length **32–40 in** (81–102 cm) after the head is knocked off; most settle around **36 in**.
- Diameter **7/8 in to 1-1/8 in** (22–28 mm). Round, straight-grained, unpainted, faintly
  gray-brown with use. Some are still varnished at the far end where nobody grips.
- The grip is **friction tape** (black cloth adhesive tape) wound 6–10 in up from the knob, or
  else nothing at all. Electrical tape is wrong for 1925 — black cloth friction tape is right.
- Good sticks are **owned**. One kid brings the stick; that kid does not get argued with as
  much. A stick that splits mid-season is a neighborhood event.
- A **taped-over crack** is authentic. So is a stick with a nail hole through it, or one with
  a name scratched into it with a penknife.
- No knob, no barrel taper, no logo, no wood grain "bat" silhouette. If the silhouette reads
  as a baseball bat at 20 feet, the model is wrong. It should read as a **stick**.

## 1.2 The ball

**Read this before you model the ball. The obvious answer is wrong by ten years.**

- `✗` **The pink Spalding High-Bounce ball — the "Spaldeen" — is not a September 1925 object.**
  Spalding began making tennis balls at Chicopee, Massachusetts in the 1920s and only later
  started selling the surplus and reject **rubber cores** as a stamped product; the ball is
  documented as manufactured **from the 1930s** and popular **1930s–1970s**. Not the word — the
  *ball*. Putting a stamped Spalding High-Bounce ball in a 1925 kid's hand is the same class of
  error as parking a Model A on the block, and it is the error this scene is most likely to make,
  because every stickball photograph a search will hand you is from 1947–1960.
- **What a 1925 kid actually has, in descending order of how defensible it is:**
  1. **A dead tennis ball with the felt worn or peeled off.** This is the ball. It is also
     *exactly the same object* as the later Spaldeen — a hollow rubber tennis-ball core — so you
     lose nothing visually and gain a decade of accuracy. Peeled felt leaves a mottled surface:
     bald patches of smooth rubber, gray-tan fuzz clinging in the seam channel, the raised seam
     line still legible. **Model the seam.** A featureless sphere is a missed opportunity.
  2. **A plain hollow rubber ball** from a candy store or a five-and-ten — unstamped, unbranded,
     red or pink or dull orange. Generic rubber toy balls are entirely period. `~` on pink
     specifically being the common color in 1925.
  3. **A ball of rags wound with string and taped.** The poorest option, lumpy, non-round, and
     it dies after two innings. Worth having on the block as the ball they use *after* the good
     one goes down the sewer.
- Size, either way: **~2-1/4 in** (57 mm), **~50 g**. Light, lively, dents when you squeeze it,
  floats a little on a long fly.
- Color: never saturated bubblegum. A dusty rose that grays with street dirt.
  New `#E88C93` → half a season old `#C58A85` → filthy `#9E7E74`. A stripped tennis core reads
  slightly duller and browner than a new toy ball. Ship at least two states.
- Rubber, so it **bounces off a parked car without denting it** and does not break windows —
  which is exactly why the game exists in this form and not another.
- **Naming caution.** *Spaldeen* is a New York pronunciation of the Spalding High-Bounce ball's
  stamped name and belongs to the late 1930s–1970s in the documented record. A 1925 kid says
  **"the ball," "the pink ball," "a rubber ball,"** or names it after a brand of tennis ball.
  `✗` for "Spaldeen" in 1925 dialogue and `✗` for a stamped Spalding ball in 1925 hands. Use the
  word in modern-day framing copy if you want it; keep it out of the block.
- The ball costs a **nickel**. Losing it down a sewer ends the game. This should be a real
  event in the game, not flavor: the grate, the reach, the coat hanger, the argument.

## 1.3 The diamond

Sewer manhole castings run down the **crown of the street**, and the gap between two of them is
the ruler for the entire sport.

**Pick a number and hold it.** Real spacing varied by neighborhood and by the age of the sewer:
in older sections of the city the castings sit roughly **100 ft** apart; on some outer-borough
streets they are **150–160 ft** apart. The stickball convention — the one all the lore is built
on, and the only one on which "four sewers" is a hit a human being could make rather than a
1,000-ft impossibility — is **~90–100 ft**. This document uses **95 ft** as the working value and
you should hard-code one number in the engine too, because a hit measured in sewers is only a
unit if the unit doesn't move. `~` on the exact figure; the *practice* of measuring in sewers is
solidly documented.

| Element | What it actually is | Dimension / placement |
|---|---|---|
| Home plate | A **manhole cover** | ~24 in clear opening, ~26 in overall cast-iron lid, flush with the crown of the road |
| Second base | The **next manhole cover** up the street | **95 ft** from home (§1.3) |
| First base | Right-side fixed object | A **parked car's right rear fender**, a **fire hydrant**, or a chalked X on the asphalt |
| Third base | Left-side fixed object | A **lamppost base**, a **cellar door**, a chalked X |
| Pitcher | Stands about halfway to second | ~45 ft; there is no mound, no rubber, nothing marked |
| Foul lines | **Chalk**, when anybody bothers | Scratched at roughly 45° off home toward the two curbs |
| Outfield | The street | Bounded by the far corner, a truck, or the point past which nobody will chase |

- The **building walls are the foul lines** for anything hit sideways. Ball off a facade = foul.
- Bases are **whatever is bolted down or parked**: car door handles, tires, hydrants, manhole
  covers, a lamppost, a coal-chute lid, a chalked mark, somebody's cap laid on the asphalt.
- The line score is kept in **chalk on the curb** or on the flank of a stoop, in a shaky
  running tally of scratches — not a scoreboard, never a scoreboard.
- Chalk is **soft white schoolroom chalk**, so the marks are thin, chalky, broken, and already
  half scuffed away. If they render as clean painted lines, they are wrong.

## 1.4 How it is played

- **Fungo / self-hit** is the oldest and simplest form and the one to lead with visually: the
  batter tosses the ball up himself and hits it. No pitcher needed, so a game can be three kids.
- **Pitched (slow-pitch / "pitching in")**: one big bounce in front of the plate off the
  asphalt, or a soft toss on the fly. This is the sociable version and the one that lets the
  camera see a duel.
- **Fast-pitch against a wall** with a strike zone chalked on brick is the third form — a
  rectangle roughly **20 in wide × 30 in tall**, bottom edge about **18 in** off the ground,
  drawn on the flattest wall on the block. Great for a one-on-one scene.
- **No gloves. Ever.** Bare hands, everybody, always. A glove on this street is an anachronism
  as loud as a cellphone.
- Fielders **play the bounce off parked cars and walls** deliberately; caroms are part of the
  geometry, not a bug. A good right fielder knows the Ford's fender kicks left.
- Teams are usually **3-on-3 to 5-on-5**. Nine is a luxury.
- **Innings** run to three outs but the game ends when the ball goes down a sewer, somebody's
  mother wins, or it gets too dark to see the pink ball — which is the real ending, every time.

## 1.5 The measure of a hitter: sewers

- A hit is measured in **sewers** — how many manhole castings past home it carries **on the
  fly**. One sewer ≈ 95 ft here (see §1.3).
- **"One sewer"** = a solid kid. **"Two sewers"** (~190 ft) = the best hitter on the block and a
  story told for years. **Three** (~285 ft) is a legend and mostly a lie.
- **The arithmetic is the argument.** If you space the castings at the real 150 ft some streets
  used, "three sewers" becomes 450 ft — a major-league home run off a broom handle — and the
  whole boast collapses. This is precisely why the kids on any given block only ever compare
  themselves to each other, and why a two-sewer man from one street is nobody on the next one
  over. Build that in: sewers are a *local* unit.
- The **"two-sewer man"** is the local hero title. It is a noun applied to a person: *"he's a
  two-sewer man."* Use it as a status label in-game — over a character's head, on a card, in
  taunts — because it is the single most specific and most portable piece of stickball language
  there is.
- **Lore caution:** "Willie Mays was a four-sewer man" is a *Harlem, 1940s* story. Beautiful,
  wrong decade. Do not put it in a 1925 kid's mouth. `✗`

## 1.6 Ground rules — the ones that make it this game and not baseball

1. **Off a wall on the fly = foul.** Tenement facades are the sidelines.
2. **Onto a stoop and back out** — a "stoop ball" — is live in some versions, dead in others.
   The point is that *this gets argued about every single time*. Make the argument the feature.
3. **Onto a fire escape and stuck there** = ground-rule double `~`, or dead ball, or a
   full-blown expedition with a broom handle. Whoever's fire escape it is has to go get it, and
   whoever's mother is home decides whether that happens.
4. **Through a window** = the game is over, everybody runs, and you can hear one kid getting
   caught. This is the block's nuclear option and worth a whole scripted event.
5. **Down the sewer** = time out, everybody at the grate, someone's arm goes in, argument about
   whose fault, someone produces a bent coat hanger or a stick with gum on the end.
6. **Under a parked car** = live, and the smallest kid goes under.
7. **Onto a roof** = home run and lost ball, both at once. Nobody is happy.
8. **Hit a car that is moving** = automatic out and everybody scatters.
9. **Ghost runners.** Not enough kids, so a runner who has to bat is left on base as an
   invisible one. Ghost runners advance exactly as far as the batter does and never score on a
   judgment call without a fight. Teams keep them by pointing: *"ghost man on second."*
10. **Do-overs.** Any interference — a car, a cop, a dog, a peddler, a mother — kills the play
    and it is replayed. The call is shouted, not discussed.

## 1.7 Interruptions, in order of frequency

| Trigger | Call | What happens on screen |
|---|---|---|
| A car turns onto the block | **"CAR!"** | Every kid drags a base out of the road, the batter steps to the curb holding the stick vertical, the car crawls through, someone slaps the fender as it passes |
| A cop on the beat | **"CHEESE IT!"** / **"COP!"** | Sticks disappear behind backs, game becomes an innocent conversation, resumes 30 seconds after he turns the corner |
| A pushcart or ice wagon | *(no call — groaning)* | Play goes around it; the cart becomes a temporary wall and the caroms change |
| Mother at a window | **"FRANKIE! UPSTAIRS!"** | One kid's shoulders drop; he is replaced by a smaller kid who has been waiting all afternoon for exactly this |
| Ball down the sewer | **"SEWER!"** | Full stop, five kids on their knees at the grate |
| Ball on a fire escape | **"IT'S ON THE SECOND FLOOR!"** | Somebody climbs; somebody's grandmother yells |
| Ball under a truck | **"UNDER!"** | The little kid goes under. Play resumes with him still coming out |
| Dusk | *(nothing)* | The pink ball goes invisible against the asphalt before the players admit it |

The **"CAR!"** interrupt is the signature beat of street stickball. Build it as a real
mechanic — a timer, a warning shout, a scatter, a resume — and half the period work is done.

## 1.8 Picking teams and settling arguments

- **Choosing up sides:** the two best players are captains by acclamation. Then either
  - **"odds or evens"** — both throw out fingers, odd/even decides who picks first; or
  - **hand-over-hand up the stick.** One captain grips the broom handle near the bottom, the
    other grips just above, alternating fists to the top. Whoever's hand covers the top end
    gets first pick. If there's not enough stick left, the last hand has to hold the tip and
    **swing the stick around his head three times** without dropping it. This is a superb
    animation and a superb piece of business — use it as the pre-game ritual every time.
- Picks go in **descending order of who can hit**, out loud, in front of everybody. The last
  kid picked is a running joke and also, always, somebody's little brother.
- **Arguments are settled by volume, then by seniority, then by who owns the ball.** There is
  no umpire. The kid who owns the ball has a permanent constitutional advantage and everyone
  resents it.
- Terminal move: **"then take your ball and go home."** Sometimes he does. That is how games
  end second-most-often.
- **"Do over"** is the universal reset and always shouted, never proposed.
- `~` **"Hindu!"** is recorded as a New York street-game do-over/time-out call. It is genuine
  street vernacular but poorly dated; safest is to use **"Do over!"** and **"Time!"** as the
  primary calls and keep "Hindu" as a rare flavor line from one older kid.

# 2. THE BLOCK

## 2.1 The lot, the massing, the numbers

New York's 1811 grid cut the island into blocks subdivided into standard lots. Everything about
a tenement block follows from that one rectangle.

| Thing | Number | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Standard lot | **25 ft wide × 100 ft deep** | The unit of the whole city. Everything is a multiple of 25 |
| Building footprint | ~**25 × 85–90 ft**, ≈ 80% lot coverage | Old Law "dumbbell" plan, pinched at the waist by air shafts |
| Stories | **5 or 6** | Six is the practical ceiling for a walk-up. The height act of **9 June 1885** capped residential buildings at **70 ft on narrow streets, 80 ft on wide ones** — a 60-ft cornice is comfortably legal either way |
| Floor-to-floor | **10–11 ft** upper floors; **11–13 ft** at the store/ground floor | |
| Cornice height | **55–62 ft** for five stories, **66–70 ft** for six | Model 60 ft and you are safe |
| Cornice projection | **2.5–3.5 ft** out from the wall, **2.5–3 ft** tall | The single biggest silhouette event on the block |
| Air shaft | ~**5 ft** between two adjoining buildings (each indents ~2 ft 6 in); ~**28 in** where one building's indent faces a solid party wall | James E. Ware's 1878 competition plan is the source of the pinch. Invisible from the street except as a dark slot at the party wall. `~` on the exact width — it varied lot to lot and got narrower the cheaper the builder |
| Apartments per floor | **4** (two front, two rear) | **14 rooms per floor** in the classic dumbbell — seven each side, running straight back, only one of them getting direct sun |
| Families per building | **20–24** | ~80–100 people in one 25-ft-wide building. The street is crowded because the buildings are |
| Street width, facade to facade | **60 ft** | Standard residential cross street |
| Sidewalk | **14–15 ft** each side | Wide. Wider than most people build it |
| Roadway | **30–32 ft** | Two lanes of parking and one and a half lanes of traffic, badly |
| Curb height | **6–8 in**, granite | Not concrete. Long gray granite curbstones with visible joints every 6–8 ft |
| **Block depth** (avenue frontage, street centerline to street centerline) | **200 ft** | ≈ 8 lots. **This is the number people misremember as "a block."** |
| **Blockface length on a cross street, avenue to avenue** | **600–920 ft** | Second Ave→Third Ave is **610 ft**; Fifth→Sixth is **920 ft**; Lexington→Third, on the later-inserted avenues, is ~**405 ft**. That is **24–36 lots** and **6–9 sewers**, not 8 lots and two |
| What you actually build | **~200 ft / 8 lots** | A *mid-block stretch*. Fine — just never let the camera or the dialogue imply that eight buildings reach from one avenue to the next. Put the avenue and the El (§2.9) far enough away that the question doesn't arise |

**Consequence for the camera:** the street canyon is 60 ft wide and 60 ft tall. It is a **1:1
canyon** — a square-section trench. That ratio is the whole visual identity. If the street
reads wider than it is tall, the block reads as a small town and the period dies.

## 2.2 Facade anatomy, bottom to top

Build one 25-ft facade as a kit and vary it; the block is 8 of them shoulder to shoulder with
**no gaps**, party wall to party wall, in a continuous unbroken wall of building.

1. **Areaway and railing (0 to –4 ft).** A sunken well in front of the basement, guarded by a
   wrought-iron railing **32–36 in** tall on a low stone curb. Bars ~5/8 in square at ~5 in
   centers. Under the stoop: a dark recess, garbage cans, a broom, a cat.
2. **Basement / cellar entry.** Down 4–5 steps under the stoop, a door with a small barred
   window. Half the buildings have a **basement shop** here instead: shoemaker, tailor,
   barber, hand laundry — a door and one window three feet below sidewalk level.
3. **Sidewalk cellar doors.** Two hinged steel leaves set flush in the sidewalk against the
   building, **4 ft × 5 ft** overall, diamond-treadplate, chained through a hasp. Rust-brown
   `#7A5A46`. These are a favorite first base.
4. **Coal chute cover.** A round cast-iron plate **12–14 in** diameter set in the sidewalk near
   the curb, with a raised lettered ring naming a foundry. The coal wagon drops a chute onto
   this and the whole block hears it.
5. **Vault lights.** Where a cellar extends under the sidewalk, a cast-iron grid **3 × 6 ft**
   inset with round **4-in glass prisms**, worn nearly smooth. Originally clear; sun-aged to
   **amethyst/lilac** `#9B84A6`. Tiny detail, enormously period-specific.
6. **Stoop (see §2.3).**
7. **Ground floor.** Either a residential entry only, or a **storefront** (§2.6).
8. **Water table / belt course.** A stone or sheet-metal band at the top of the ground floor
   separating store from dwelling, painted to match the trim.
9. **Windows, floors 2–5.** Double-hung wood sash, **2-over-2** or **4-over-4** lights.
   Opening **2 ft 8 in – 3 ft 0 in wide × 5 ft 6 in – 6 ft 0 in tall**. Four windows per floor
   across a 25 ft facade, on roughly **6 ft 3 in centers**, leaving ~18 in of brick at the
   party walls. Sash painted dark green, dark red-brown, or black; almost never white.
10. **Lintels and sills.** Every window head carries a **projecting lintel** — brownstone, or
    cast iron, or (1880s+) galvanized pressed metal painted to imitate brownstone. Projection
    **3–5 in**, height **8–12 in**, often with a molded cap and carved rosettes or swags. Sills
    are brownstone, **4–6 in** thick, projecting **2–3 in**, worn and often cracked.
    **The lintel shadow line is the most legible period signature on the whole facade.** Model
    it as real geometry, not a texture. Four lintels × five floors × eight buildings = 160
    little horizontal shadows, and that rhythm is what says "New York, 1880s tenement."
11. **Brick field.** Common red brick, running bond, **8 in × 2-1/4 in** face with 3/8-in
    joints. That is **2-5/8 in per course → ~4.5 courses per foot** (3 courses ≈ 8 in) — the
    "4 courses per foot" in the previous draft was wrong and would have stretched a five-story
    facade by nearly three feet of brick. Across a 60-ft wall that is **~275 visible courses**.
    Mortar is gray-buff, and four decades of coal soot have darkened the whole wall unevenly,
    worst under the cornice, under the sills, and around the windows where smoke leaves.
12. **Fire escape (§2.4).** Bolted onto the facade, dead center, covering the middle two
    windows on every floor from 2 up.
13. **Cornice.** Pressed galvanized sheet metal on brackets: a heavy projecting shelf with
    **6–10 scrolled brackets** across 25 ft, a dentil or egg-and-dart band, a frieze. Painted
    a dull brown or a dark red to imitate stone. Dented, streaked with rust at the seams,
    pigeons on it. This is the block's skyline, and it must be **irregular building to
    building** — different heights within a couple of feet, different bracket counts, different
    paint. A uniform cornice line reads as one extruded prefab and kills the block.
14. **Parapet and roof.** Behind the cornice: a low brick parapet **2–3 ft** tall, flat tar
    roof, and the roofscape of §2.7.

## 2.3 Stoops

- The stoop is the **social organ of the block** and the seating for the entire spectator crowd.
- **Height of the parlor floor above the sidewalk: 4–6 ft** for a tenement (a fancier brownstone
  row house goes to 8–10 ft with a 10–15 riser stoop; a tenement is humbler).
- **Steps: 7 to 10 risers.** Riser **7–7.5 in**, tread **11–12 in**. Stone (brownstone or
  bluestone), worn into a shallow dish in the middle of each tread.
- **Width: 5–6 ft** between cheek walls. Cheek walls are solid stone, 12–16 in thick, with a
  flat top — the preferred seat for a teenager who is too cool to sit on the steps.
- **Iron railings** on top of the cheek walls, **30–34 in** tall, or none at all on a plain one.
- **Landing** at the top, **4–5 ft** deep, then the double entry doors: wood, paneled, with a
  transom above and a bell panel of brass buttons and paper name slips beside them.
- **Newel posts** at the bottom, stone or cast iron, ~12 in square, capped. Somebody's cap is
  on one. A ball can carom off a newel at a stupid angle.
- Stoops **project 4–6 ft into the 14-ft sidewalk**, leaving 8–10 ft of clear walk. That
  projection is what makes the sidewalk a series of alcoves rather than a corridor, and it is
  why the crowd reads as clusters instead of a line.

## 2.4 Fire escapes

New York put its fire escapes on the **street facade**, which is the reason a New York block
looks like a New York block and a Chicago or Boston block does not. Get this right and half the
identity is done.

- **Balcony: 3 ft 0 in to 3 ft 4 in deep**, **7–9 ft wide** — enough to cover two window
  openings. **Hard statutory ceiling: no fire escape may project more than 4 ft 6 in into the
  public highway from the lot line** (NY Multiple Dwelling Law §53). So balcony + railing +
  everything hanging off it must fit inside 4 ft 6 in. If your fire escape reads as a 6-ft
  balcony, it is illegal and it looks illegal.
- **Deck:** open bar grating or perforated plate, ~1/4 in bars at ~1 in centers. Light passes
  through it, which means it casts a **striped shadow** on the facade beneath, and the whole
  assembly reads as lace, not as a solid shelf. This is critical: a solid-plate fire escape
  looks like a balcony and kills it.
- **Railing: 30–36 in** tall, flat bar top rail, vertical pickets, sometimes a decorative
  scroll or a run of cast rosettes at the mid-rail on the fancier buildings.
- **Stairs between levels:** straight runs at **~45–60°**, tread width ~18–20 in, mounted at
  alternating ends of the balcony so the stair zig-zags up the facade. That zig-zag is the
  visual signature — never stack them in one vertical line.
- **Drop ladder:** from the lowest balcony, a fixed or counterbalanced stair/ladder that swings
  down toward the sidewalk. **Statute: every part of a fire escape must be at least 10 ft above
  any sidewalk directly below it** (MDL §53). So build the rest position at **10–12 ft**, not 8 —
  10 ft is the floor, not the average. That clearance is out of reach of a kid, which is
  precisely why a kid climbs the areaway railing to jump for it, and why a ball stuck on the
  second-floor balcony is a whole expedition. Width ~15 in `~`.
- **Load:** the code wants balconies and stairs of iron or stone good for **80 lb per square
  foot**. Which means five kids on one can stand there. Which means five kids do.
- **Color:** painted black, or dark red-brown primer, or bare rusted iron. `#2B2724` black,
  `#6E4231` red oxide, `#8A6A54` rust bloom. Every building on the block should differ.
- **What lives on a fire escape in September 1925:** a wooden crate, a milk bottle, a geranium
  in a coffee can, a pillow airing over the rail, a mop, a birdcage, a mattress somebody slept
  on in August and has not yet dragged in, a cat, laundry.

## 2.5 Ash cans, garbage, and the gutter

- **Ash cans:** galvanized steel cans **20 in diameter × 26 in tall** with two side handles and
  a domed lid, dented, standing in twos and threes at the curb or under the stoop. Every flat
  burns coal, so ash is the block's main waste stream and the cans are always half full of
  gray-white clinker.
- **Gutters** carry a permanent line of grit, straw, cinders, horse leavings, cabbage leaves,
  and newspaper. Not filth — *use*. The street is swept but never clean.
- The **street cleaner** with a broom and a two-wheel can cart is a real and recurring figure,
  and he is not amused by the game. Two things to letter correctly on his cart, his can, and his
  badge: in 1925 the agency is the **DEPARTMENT OF STREET CLEANING** — the Department of
  Sanitation does not exist until **1929**, and "DSNY" on any 1925 prop is a four-year error.
  The white duck uniform that earned the nickname **"White Wing"** ran from 1895 into the 1920s;
  by September 1925 it may already be giving way to a darker working uniform, so `~` on the
  white — keep the nickname in the kids' mouths either way, since the name outlived the cloth.
- Ash and garbage leave the block by **horse cart**, not truck. Motor collection is coming but
  the horse is still doing most of it.

## 2.6 Storefronts (ground floor)

A tenement block has a store every two or three buildings, always at the corners and often
mid-block. The storefront occupies the full 25 ft width and stands **11–13 ft** tall.

- **Bulkhead** (below the display glass): wood panels or pressed metal, **18–30 in** tall,
  painted dark green, oxblood, or black; scuffed to bare wood at kick height.
- **Display glass:** one or two big plate lights, **6–7 ft tall**, with a wooden mullion.
  Gold-leaf lettering on the inside face of the glass (§4.3).
- **Transom** above the glass: a horizontal band of small panes, sometimes **prism glass** with
  a ribbed face to throw daylight to the back of the shop — another quiet 1900–1925 signature.
- **Recessed entry:** the door sits back **3–5 ft** from the sidewalk between angled show
  windows, with a **tile or terrazzo floor** in the recess — often a hexagonal white tile field
  with the shop's initial or name laid in black tile. A superb detail almost nobody builds.
- **Sign band** above the transom: a flat board or metal panel **20–30 in** tall running the
  full 25 ft, hand-lettered (§4).
- **Awning:** canvas, **striped** (broad 8–10 in stripes) or plain, on a pipe frame, projecting
  **5–7 ft**, valance scalloped and hanging **7 ft** above the sidewalk. Rolled up on a crank
  in the morning, down over the west-facing shops on a September afternoon. Colors: faded
  green-and-cream, maroon-and-cream, ochre-and-white. Sun-bleached on the top face and darker
  underneath — bake that into the texture.
- **Goods on the sidewalk:** crates of produce on trestles, a barrel of pickles, a rack of
  brooms, a stand of newspapers, sacks of onions. Stores spill 3–4 ft onto the walk, always.

## 2.7 The roofscape ("Tar Beach")

Visible over the cornice line whenever the camera lifts. This is free period value.

- **Chimneys:** brick stacks **3–6 ft** above the parapet at the party walls, each crowned with
  **2 to 8 terracotta chimney pots** — cylindrical, **8–12 in** diameter, **18–30 in** tall,
  in orange-buff `#B8724A`. A cluster of pots against the sky is one of the strongest 1920s
  city silhouettes available and costs almost nothing to model.
- **Water tanks:** cedar or redwood staves bound with iron hoops, conical roof. The New York
  tank-builders' names to letter on a stencil or a signboard if you ever need one are
  **Rosenwach** and **Isseks Brothers**, both period-correct family firms. `~`
  **10–12 ft diameter × 10–12 ft tall**, holding ~10,000 gallons, on a steel or timber frame
  lifting them **15–20 ft above the roof**. Wood weathers **silver-gray** `#8E8579` with darker
  streaks under the hoops. **Reality check:** tanks are required where city pressure can't
  reach — roughly **above six stories**. A block of five-story walk-ups would *not* each have
  one. Put tanks on the taller buildings behind and to the side, on the corner building, and in
  the middle distance, where they belong. Twelve tanks over eight five-story tenements is
  wrong and a knowledgeable critic will say so.
- **Bulkheads:** a small brick or wood roof-stair house, **6 × 8 ft footprint × 8 ft tall**,
  with a slanted door.
- **Skylights:** low pyramidal wire-glass hoods over the stairwell, ~4 × 6 ft.
- **Pigeon coops:** a wood-and-chicken-wire loft **6–10 ft long × 4–5 ft tall** on the roof of
  one building, with a landing board and a man on the roof waving a long bamboo pole with a rag
  on it to keep the flock circling. This is real, it is New York, and it gives the sky motion.
- **Clotheslines**, TV-antenna-free sky, water tanks, chimney pots, pigeons, and one chair.

## 2.8 Laundry lines — how they are actually strung

Almost every reference gets this wrong, and it is very visible.

- Lines run **from a rear window across the back yard to a pole or to the opposite building** —
  the classic dense laundry canyon is a **rear-yard** condition, not a street-facade one.
- On the **street facade**, laundry appears on **fire escapes** (draped over the railing, or on
  a short line strung between the fire escape and a window jamb) — not on long spans across the
  roadway. A cat's cradle of ropes across the street over a stickball game looks glorious and
  is a **Hollywood invention**. `✗` — see §9.
- Mechanism: a **pulley clothesline**. Two galvanized pulleys, one screwed to the window frame,
  one on a pole or the far wall, a continuous loop of rope, and a **line-prop** or spreader.
  The woman stands at the window and hauls the loop hand over hand.
- **Wooden spring clothespins** and **one-piece slotted peg pins**; both existed by 1925.
- What is on the line, September, working-class: white sheets, union suits, men's shirts,
  aprons, dish towels, a child's nightgown, black stockings. Whites dominate — this is a wash
  day, and colors were washed separately and less often.
- **Backlit laundry is the single best free lighting moment in the scene** (§6.4). A white sheet
  with the low sun behind it goes translucent, glows, and shows the shadow of the line and the
  pins through it.

## 2.9 The far end of the block — the El

If the block terminates on an avenue carrying an elevated line, put the El **at the end of the
street**, not over the game. It should be a **presence at the end of the corridor**: a dark
steel lattice crossing the mouth of the street, a train passing every couple of minutes,
strobing light through the girders, and the sound arriving before and after it is seen.

- Structure: paired **steel columns at the curb lines**, riveted lattice or plate, ~**40–50 ft
  apart** longitudinally, carrying transverse girders and a deck.
- **Deck underside roughly 20–25 ft above the street** `~` — a low ceiling, and low enough that
  the second-floor windows of the buildings alongside look straight into passing trains. The
  avenue beneath is in permanent brown twilight, and the shops under it (pawnbroker, second-hand
  furniture, cafeteria, shoeshine) live in that gloom.
- **Which El.** Name it. In September 1925 Manhattan has four: the **Second Avenue**, **Third
  Avenue**, **Sixth Avenue** and **Ninth Avenue** elevateds, all IRT-operated. An East Harlem
  cross street running to Third Avenue gets the **Third Avenue El**, which is the right answer
  for the default block and the one that lasted longest.
  `✗` Do not put a **subway** entrance at the end of this street unless you have checked the
  line: the IRT and BMT exist in 1925, the **IND does not** (1932).
- Painted a dull dark green-black or red-brown, rust-bloomed, dripping. Elevated columns are
  bolted through the sidewalk with a wide cast base and a permanent oily halo.
- The El is a **light effect** as much as a structure: horizontal bars of shadow, and the sudden
  clatter-and-flicker of a train that reduces everyone on screen to shouting.

# 3. STREET FURNITURE, PAVING, AND VEHICLES

## 3.1 Paving — and where each kind actually appears

This is a top-three period tell and it is usually wrong.

| Surface | Where | Look |
|---|---|---|
| **Sheet asphalt** | **Most residential cross streets by 1925.** This is your game surface | Dark gray-brown, patched in mismatched shades, cracked into a fine map, oil-stained, worn to the aggregate at the crown. `#4A474A` base with patches `#3A3B3E` and `#5C5348` |
| **Belgian block** | Industrial/waterfront streets, market streets, alleys, and under the El. **Manhattan still had ~140 miles of it in 1949** (against ~15 today), so in 1925 it is everywhere you look sideways | **Tooled rectangular granite setts, ~5 in wide** — the width was proportioned to a horseshoe — **× 8–10 in long**, laid in courses or arcs, joints of tar or sand, humped in the middle, greasy when wet. Laid from the 1850s; the surviving tooled type from the 1870s |
| **Cobblestone (true)** | Almost nowhere by 1925 | Rounded natural stones. If your street reads as rounded river stones, it is a century early. `✗` |
| **Granite block over trolley routes** | Avenues with car tracks | Blocks kept where rails needed maintenance access |
| **Brick paving** | A handful of outer-borough streets | Red-brown, laid in herringbone |
| **Sidewalk** | Everywhere | **Bluestone flags** on the older blocks — big gray-blue slabs 3–5 ft on a side with visible joints — giving way to poured concrete in ~5 × 5 ft squares with a tooled edge. Mix both on one block. Concrete is stamped with the contractor's name at the corners |

**Best answer for the stickball street: asphalt, patched, over an older Belgian block base that
shows through at the gutter line and in one worn-out patch mid-block.** That single detail —
granite blocks surfacing through broken asphalt — is worth ten generic textures.

## 3.2 Manhole covers and grates

- **Sewer manhole:** cast iron, **~24 in opening / 26 in overall**, ~250–300 lb, set in a
  cast frame flush with the crown of the road. Face pattern is a **raised geometric grid** —
  squares, hexagons, concentric rings, or a waffle — plus raised letters around the rim.
  Wear-polished silver-gray on the high points `#8C8C8A`, rust-black in the recesses `#3A3129`.
- Period lettering you can cast onto covers: `SEWER`, `CROTON`, `D.P.W.`, `DEPT. OF WATER
  SUPPLY GAS & ELECTRICITY`, `B.O.S. — BUREAU OF SEWERS`, `N.Y. TEL. CO.` Dates cast in: `1874`,
  `1898`, `1907`.
- **Foundry names — use these, they are documented New York iron founders whose covers survive:**
  `J. B. & J. M. CORNELL` (26th St & 11th Ave, firm back to 1828; also cast Central Park's iron
  bridges), `CORNELL IRON WORKS`, `JACOB MARK` / `JACOB MARK SONS` (Worth St — their covers carry
  a six-pointed star with colored glass inserts, which is a gorgeous thing to model), and
  `MARCY FOUNDRY 14 BEEKMAN ST.` The `ABBOTT HARDWARE CO.` in the previous draft could not be
  substantiated and has been cut — do not letter unverifiable foundries onto a prop the camera
  lives on.
- **Electric-company covers:** by 1925 the operating company is **`THE NEW YORK EDISON CO.`**
  The `EDISON ELECTRIC ILLUMINATING CO.` name was folded into New York Edison in **1901**, so an
  Edison Electric Illuminating cover is a *survivor* — correct only as an old, worn, out-of-date
  lid among newer ones, never as the standard. That mixture of dates underfoot is the point.
- **Home plate is one of these**, and the camera will be on it constantly. Model it as real
  geometry with a real bevel, give it a worn brass-gray sheen where a thousand feet have
  polished the pattern, and let the chalk marks around it be smeared by the batter's shoes.
- **Sewer grates / catch basins:** rectangular cast-iron grates **2 × 3 ft** set into the gutter
  at the curb, bars running lengthwise with ~1-1/2 in slots. The ball fits. That is the point.

## 3.3 Lamp posts

- **Bishop's crook**, the New York lamppost — appearing from about **1892**, credited as a
  design of **1896** to Richard Rogers Bowker of the Edison company, cast by **J. L. Mott Iron
  Works** among others, and everywhere by 1925.
- **Heights (the previous draft contradicted itself here and is corrected):** the **cast-iron
  shaft is about 13 ft**; the curved top above it is bent pipe with cast fittings applied, and
  the whole post stands **~15–17 ft to the top of the crook**, with the luminaire hanging at the
  end of the arc roughly **12–14 ft** above the sidewalk. A post whose *total* height is 12 ft
  cannot have a 13-ft cast-iron shaft, and a 12-ft lamppost is a garden light. `~` on the exact
  luminaire drop; **13 ft of cast iron is the documented anchor** — hang everything off that.
- The shaft is **fluted**, rising from a heavy stepped base **~16–18 in** diameter, and carries
  a **cast garland / acanthus motif wrapping the shaft** just below the crook. The crook itself
  is a single sweeping quarter-turn with a **scrolled tail and a leaf ornament** in the curl.
- The **luminaire** hangs from the tip: a teardrop or bell-shaped glass shade in a cast frame.
- **Painted dark green or black.** Green is the more common city standard; black reads better
  against brick. `#26332B` green, `#211F1D` black.
- Set at the **curb line**, one at each corner and one mid-block. **Third base.**
- A lamppost also carries: a **street-name sign** (a small enameled plate or a cast bracket), a
  **fire alarm indicator light** if it is the corner post, and a **kid hanging off it**.

## 3.4 Fire hydrants ("Johnny pumps")

- Cast iron, **~30 in tall** above ground, a fluted or plain barrel **7–9 in** diameter on a
  wider flanged base, a domed bonnet with a **pentagon operating nut** on top, and **two side
  outlets with chained caps** plus a larger steamer connection.
- Set **12–18 in back from the curb face**, on a small stone or concrete pad.
- **Painted** — 1920s hydrants are commonly a single body color, and the modern national
  color-coding scheme does not apply. Safe choices: **dull red-oxide** `#7C3B2E`, **black**
  `#242220`, or **silver-gray aluminum paint** `#9A9A96`, all chipped down to rust. Vary them
  along the block; a whole street of identical fresh red hydrants is a modern look. `✗`
- **Call it a Johnny pump.** Lower Manhattan and Brooklyn vernacular, and the term's earliest
  solid print citation is **Henry Roth's *Call It Sleep* (1934)** — a novel set on the Lower East
  Side around 1911–13, i.e. written *about* exactly this world by someone who grew up in it,
  which is why it is usable here. Folk etymology traces it to a compressed-air hydrant by the
  inventor John Giraud, 1830. `~` for 1925 speech, but very well founded. It is first base.
- In summer, opened with a wrench for the block to play in. In September, dry, with a rusty
  drool stain down the gutter and a chalk mark on the curb beside it.

## 3.5 Fire alarm boxes

- **Gamewell** cast-iron box on a **fluted cast-iron pedestal**, corner-mounted. Total height
  **4 ft 6 in – 5 ft**; box body ~**14 in wide × 18 in tall × 8 in deep**.
- **Painted red**, with a small **ruby glass globe or lamp on top** so it can be found at night,
  and hand-painted gold or white lettering on the door.
- Lettering — the real instruction panel reads, in four stacked lines:
  `FOR FIRE` / `BREAK THE GLASS` / `OPEN THE DOOR` / `PULL HOOK DOWN ONCE AND LET GO`.
  Plus `FIRE ALARM`, `POLICE`, and a **box number** cast or painted on it — the number is the
  whole system: it is tapped out on a bell at the central office and tells the firehouse which
  corner. The pedestal often carries a torch finial or a flat cap.
- Perfect world-anchoring prop at the corner where cars enter the block.

## 3.6 Mailboxes

- **Olive drab green.** The sequence: collection boxes went **dark green from 1909** (to stop
  people confusing them with fire and emergency equipment), shifted to **olive drab after the
  First World War**, and stayed olive drab until **4 July 1955**, when Postmaster General Arthur
  Summerfield announced the change to red, white and blue; the familiar all-blue box is **1971**.
  So September 1925 is olive drab, `#5A5B45`, chalky and chipped. **A red-white-and-blue or a
  blue mailbox in 1925 is a thirty-year error.** `✗`
  (The charming story that the Army donated surplus olive drab is folklore — Post Office records
  show paint bought on the open market. Correct color, wrong anecdote; don't put the anecdote in
  a tooltip.)
- Form: a **cast-iron pedestal box**, rounded-top body ~**18 in wide × 15 in deep × 24 in tall**
  on a single fluted post, total ~**4 ft**, with an angled pull-down chute door and a small
  collection-times card in a frame. `U. S. MAIL` and `LETTERS` embossed on the body.
- On lamp-post-mounted variants, a smaller box strapped to the pole at 4 ft.

## 3.7 Other street furniture

- **Hitching posts and iron carriage steps** survive in front of a few of the older buildings —
  a short cast-iron post 3–4 ft with a ring, or a stone block at the curb. Vestigial by 1925,
  and the more valuable for it.
- **Newsstand:** a plywood-and-canvas kiosk at the corner or under the El, **6 × 4 ft**, hung
  three deep with papers and magazines held down by lengths of pipe. Papers, all live in
  September 1925: *The Daily News* (founded 1919, tabloid, picture front page — the one the kids
  read), *The Evening World*, *The New York American*, *The Sun*, *The Evening Graphic* (started
  1924, lurid, faked photographs), *Il Progresso Italo-Americano*, *The Jewish Daily Forward*
  (Yiddish masthead, right-to-left), *The New York Amsterdam News* in Harlem. Two cents.
  A front page that week would be carrying the coal strike and the primary.
- **Barber pole:** a helically striped cylinder **3–4 ft tall × 6–8 in diameter** in a bracket
  by the door, **red and white with blue added in America**, with a bulbous cap and base.
  Motorized rotating poles existed by the 1920s — a slow turn is period-correct and free motion.
- **Sidewalk clock** on a cast-iron post outside a jeweler, ~15 ft tall, two-faced, gilt hands.
- **Horse trough** or a public drinking fountain at a corner, granite, often with a small basin
  at ground level for dogs.
- **Telephone / telegraph poles** with crossarms and glass insulators survive on outer-borough
  streets; Manhattan's wires went underground in the 1890s after the blizzard, so **do not
  festoon a Manhattan street with overhead wires.** `✗`
- **Signposts:** small enameled steel street-name plates, white letters on a dark ground,
  bracketed to the lamp post; not the big green modern blades.

## 3.8 Vehicles — silhouettes, colors, behavior

The block should hold **6–10 parked vehicles and 1–2 moving ones**. Parked cars are outfield
walls, dugouts, backstops, and bases; they are gameplay geometry, not scenery.

**Ford Model T (1908–1927) — the default car on this street.**
- **Length ~10 ft 8 in (128 in)** for the 1925 touring car — the previous draft's 11 ft 4 in was
  too long. **Width ~5 ft 6 in** over the body, **~5 ft 8 in** over the hubcaps, which are the
  widest thing on the car. **Height just under 7 ft with the top up.** **Wheelbase 100 in.**
  The proportion that matters: it is **taller than it is wide**, and it is short. Tall, narrow
  and stubby — not long and low. Get that wrong and it reads as a 1930s sedan at any distance.
- **Wood artillery spokes**, **30 × 3-1/2 in** clincher tires — **very thin**, with a lot of
  daylight under the running boards and between the wheel and the fender. Wire wheels arrive as
  an option with the "improved" 1926 cars; on this block in September 1925 they are wrong or, at
  most, on one brand-new car nobody can stop talking about. `~`
- Silhouette: a vertical radiator shell, a flat two-piece windshield, a **black canvas top**
  with a squared-off cab, exposed running boards, a spare on the back, big separate fenders
  with a deep valley between fender and body.
- **Color: black.** From 1914 to 1925 the Model T was essentially black only; Ford brought
  colors back with the 1926–27 cars (green, maroon, gray). For September 1925: **black, black,
  black, with one older/repainted oddball** — a hand-brushed dark green or maroon on a 1919 car —
  for variety. The 1926 models begin reaching dealers in the autumn of 1925, so a single
  factory-colored Ford is *just* arguable and should be treated as a deliberate, singular choice,
  never as the default. `~`
- Weathering does the work: dust-gray on the lower body, mud in the fender valleys, a dulled
  chalky black on the horizontal surfaces, brass or nickel that has gone dark.
- The **Model A does not exist yet** (Dec 1927). Its 1928 palette — Niagara Blue, Arabian Sand,
  Thorne Brown — is three years in the future. Using it is a common and catchable error. `✗`

**Trucks and commercial vehicles.**
- **Ford Model TT** and **Mack AB / Bulldog** stake-bed and box trucks: **hard rubber tires**,
  chain or worm drive, **open cab with no doors** and a canvas side curtain, wooden stake bed,
  hand-painted signwriting on the door and body panel. Speeds of 15 mph, top out at 25.
- Color: the **body is the advertisement**. Bright, saturated, hand-lettered — a green truck
  with gold-and-red shaded lettering, a red truck with cream lettering (§4.6).
- **Ice wagon:** horse-drawn, an enclosed wooden box body with a canvas flap at the back,
  sawdust inside, water dripping onto the asphalt in a dark trail behind it. The **iceman**
  carries a **20–40 lb block on a leather shoulder cape with tongs**, and the kids follow the
  wagon to grab chips off the tailgate. A whole scripted vignette.
- **Milk wagon:** horse-drawn, light-painted, glass bottles in wire crates, out before dawn.
  Sheffield Farms and Borden's are the New York names.
- **Coal truck / wagon:** filthy, black, a chute clamped to the side, two men with a canvas
  hood over their shoulders and faces black to the eyes.
- **Horses are still on the street in 1925** — for ice, milk, coal, produce, junk, and rags.
  About one in four commercial vehicles on a poor block. A patient draft horse standing in the
  traces with a feed bag on and one hip cocked is a period lock, and it is free aliveness.
- **Pushcarts:** wooden, **6 ft long × 3 ft wide**, two large iron-tired wheels **~3 ft
  diameter** at the middle, two handles and a prop leg. Loaded with produce in wooden crates,
  or fish on ice, or hats, or suspenders, or fifty pairs of shoes. Often with a canvas parasol
  or a bent-pipe awning, a set of hanging scales, and an oil lamp for dusk. **The peddler-licence
  count was frozen at 6,000 in 1914 and was still capped there through the 1920s**, with a large
  unlicensed trade on top of it — by 1930 the business supported some 47,000 family members, over
  half of it on the Lower East Side. **One or two carts on the block, at the corner, is right; a
  solid market wall is a different street** (Hester, Orchard, First Avenue) and should be used
  deliberately, not by default.
- **Bicycles:** delivery bikes with a big front basket and a shop's name on a plate under the
  crossbar; a boy on one is a fine bit of background traffic.

## 3.9 Trolleys — the detail nobody gets right

- Manhattan **banned overhead trolley wires**. Manhattan streetcars ran on an **underground
  conduit**: a **narrow continuous slot between the rails**, from which a "plow" hanging under
  the car picked up current. **So: rails and a slot in the pavement, and NO overhead wire, NO
  trolley pole, NO catenary on a Manhattan avenue.** `✗` for overhead wire in Manhattan.
- **Brooklyn, Queens, and much of the Bronx did use overhead wire** with a trolley pole and
  span wires. If your block is Brooklyn, wires are correct and welcome.
- Track detail: two rails set flush in granite blocks or asphalt, **4 ft 8-1/2 in gauge**, with
  the conduit slot centered between them, everything worn to a polished silver line.
- Trolleys belong on the **avenue at the end of the block**, not on the game street.
- Buses (double-deck Fifth Avenue Coach open-tops, and the new closed single-deckers) exist and
  belong on avenues.

# 4. SIGNAGE AND TYPOGRAPHY

## 4.1 The governing principle

**Everything on this block was lettered by a human being with a brush.** There is no vinyl, no
extruded plastic, no backlit box, no printed banner, no logo lockup used at three sizes. Every
sign is a one-off, slightly irregular, optically spaced rather than metrically spaced, and
shaded or outlined by hand. Two signs by the same painter on the same block will share a
personality; two by different painters will not agree about anything.

Density is high: a New York block carries **far more lettering than a modeller's instinct
allows.** Sign band, transom, window glass, door glass, awning valance, bulkhead, the whole
side wall of the corner building, the truck parked out front, the pushcart, the ash can, and
the manhole cover all have words on them. **Under-lettering the block is the most common way a
period city scene reads generic.**

## 4.2 The typographic palette (five faces, no more)

1. **Fat Egyptian / slab serif, condensed, all caps.** The workhorse of wall ads and sign bands.
   Heavy square slabs, tight fit, often shaded. Think `Clarendon`/`French Clarendon` energy.
2. **Grotesque / Gothic sans, condensed, all caps.** Bold, plain, and everywhere on price signs
   and civic lettering. Very slight stroke contrast, flat terminals.
3. **Copperplate Gothic** (1901) — tiny wedge serifs on a sans skeleton, wide letterspacing.
   The face of professional respectability: doctors, notaries, jewelers, banks, undertakers.
4. **Cheltenham** (1896–1900s) — the newspaper-and-advertising serif of the era. Sturdy, short
   ascenders. Correct for anything printed: newspapers, posters, handbills.
5. **Brush script / Spencerian casual** for one or two shop names and for a soda-fountain sign.
   Also **gold-leaf ornamental script** on window glass.
6. Sparingly, for a brand-new shop or a movie house: an early **geometric Art Deco** face
   (Kabel is 1927 — just late; use a hand-drawn geometric instead of naming a face).

**Composition rules a sign painter followed and you should:**
- Lines are **centered and justified to fill the panel width** — the painter stretched or
  condensed each line to fit, so line lengths match and letter widths vary line to line.
- **The most important word is the biggest by a lot** — a 3:1 or 4:1 size jump, not 1.2:1.
- Words are **all caps** unless the shop is trying to look genteel.
- Numbers and prices get a **¢ sign** and are often the second-biggest thing on the sign.
- Letters get **a hard drop shadow, an outline, or a bevel-and-shade** in two extra colors.
- Palettes are **two or three colors plus gold**: cream on dark green; gold on black; white on
  oxblood; black and red on cream; gold on red.

## 4.3 Gold-leaf window lettering

The mark of a shop with pretensions. **23-karat gold leaf applied to the inside face of the
plate glass**, water-gilded so it reads as a mirror-bright surface, then backed up with paint.
Usually **outlined in black and often shaded in a second color**, arched or in a straight line
across the upper third of the glass, with a fine gold rule above and below. Common on: druggist,
jeweler, tailor, insurance agent, notary, dentist, doctor, undertaker, saloon-turned-lunchroom.
Reads at 30 ft as a bright warm-gold ribbon on a dark window, and catches the low sun.

## 4.4 Wall ads (ghost signs)

Painted directly onto brick by **"wall dogs"** working from a swing stage — a whole
building-sized ad in a day or two. Present as three layers of history on the same wall:
1. A fresh, saturated ad on the flat blank party wall exposed where the next lot is lower.
2. A **half-faded** older ad beneath it, showing through.
3. A **ghost** — a nearly illegible third ad, readable only as pale letter shapes.
Paint sinks into the brick, so the lettering follows the courses and joints, and weather eats it
from the top down and from the windward corner in. Whites survive; reds fade first to pink.

## 4.6 Truck and cart lettering

Door panel, in three lines, arched top line, centered: **name, trade, address and telephone
exchange.** Telephone numbers in 1925 New York are an **exchange name plus four digits** —
`HARLEM 4417`, `ORCHARD 2960`, `MURRAY HILL 8100`, `TREMONT 1140`. That format is a free,
tiny, unfakeable period signal. Letters are gold or cream with a **red drop shadow and a fine
dark outline**, plus a hairline pinstripe panel around the whole block of text.

# 5. PEOPLE

## 5.1 The kids — what they wear

Ages 8 to 14, mostly boys on the field, mostly hand-me-downs, everything a size wrong.

- **Cap.** A **newsboy / eight-panel flat cap** in wool tweed — the single most identifying
  garment. Worn pushed back, sideways, or backwards while batting. Colors: heather gray-brown
  `#6B6154`, olive `#5E5B44`, brown herringbone `#6A5747`, a faded navy `#3C4353`. Some kids
  bareheaded, one with a cap two sizes too big.
- **Knickers** (knickerbockers) — trousers gathered and **buckled just below or just above the
  knee**, in wool or corduroy, baggy at the seat and thigh. Brown, gray, navy, olive. This is
  the second identifying garment, and the buckle-at-the-knee silhouette is unmistakable.
- **Long stockings** — black or dark tan wool, pulled to the knee, sagging around the calf,
  darned at the heel. Or, on the poorer kids, **bare legs** and dirty knees.
- **Shirt** — collarless or with a soft collar, off-white/ecru, sleeves rolled to the elbow,
  worn open at the throat, shirttail out. Never a t-shirt with a print. Never a graphic.
- **Suspenders (braces)** — narrow, over the shirt, one strap frequently off the shoulder.
  Belts exist but suspenders read faster and are more period.
- **Sweater or vest** — a shapeless wool pullover or a buttoned cardigan, elbows gone.
- **Boots/shoes** — leather **lace-up high-tops** to the ankle, scuffed gray, laces knotted
  where they broke, soles separating at the toe on at least one kid. Occasionally
  canvas-and-rubber sneakers on a kid whose family has a few dollars — Keds existed from 1916,
  so one pair on the block is defensible and marks that kid as well-off.
- **Nobody is clean.** Knees, elbows, cuffs, and the seat of the pants are the dirt zones.
- **Girls** on the block, watching or playing potsy: a dropped-waist cotton dress to mid-calf,
  long stockings, a hair ribbon or a bob, a sweater. A girl who plays and is good at it is
  historically real and should exist in the roster.
- **Silhouette rule for the modeller:** cap + open collar + suspenders + baggy knickers +
  saggy stockings + ankle boots. Five reads, all at the silhouette level, all in one figure.

## 5.2 The adults, and where they are

- **On the stoop:** two women in housedresses and aprons, sleeves rolled, one with a baby on
  her hip, sitting on the top step with their knees together and their forearms on their knees.
  An old man in shirtsleeves, vest, and a flat cap on the cheek wall with a folded newspaper.
- **In the windows:** a woman leaning out on a folded towel or a pillow laid over the sill,
  elbows down, watching the street. **Two or three lit windows with a leaning figure is the
  cheapest and best aliveness on the whole facade.**
- **Men, working:** the iceman, the coal men, a peddler, the street sweeper (§2.5), and a cop on
  the beat in a dark **double-breasted, high-collar wool tunic with two rows of brass buttons**,
  a shield on the breast, a **peaked cap**, and a nightstick on a leather thong.
  `✗` **No domed custodian helmet.** American forces, New York's included, had given the London
  helmet up by the early years of the century; by 1925 it is a peaked cap and has been for a
  generation. The previous draft offered the helmet as an option and it is cut. He is also on
  foot — motor patrol and the radio car belong to the 1930s.
- **Men, idle (three o'clock on a Tuesday):** in shirtsleeves and vest, sleeve garters, no
  jacket, cap or straw boater (a boater is correct through Labor Day; by late September it
  should mostly be gone — one stubborn old man still in his is a good joke), leaning on a
  hydrant, arguing about baseball.
- **Older boys, 16–18:** long trousers, not knickers. The switch from knickers to long pants is
  the entire adolescence marker of the era, and it is a story beat: the kid who got long pants
  this year won't play with the little kids any more, and is sitting on a stoop pretending not
  to watch.
- Everyone wears a hat outdoors. Everyone.

## 5.3 The block's mix

The stickball block is an immigrant working-class block, and it is never one nationality.

- **Italian** — Southern Italian and Sicilian; East Harlem above 106th east of Lexington,
  Mulberry Street downtown, Belmont in the Bronx. Feast societies, salumerie, the language on
  the street, grandmothers in black.
- **Eastern European Jewish** — the Lower East Side, Brownsville, the East Bronx. Yiddish
  signage, hand laundries, the *Forward*, and a candy store on every third corner.
- **Irish** — Hell's Kitchen, the West Side, parts of the Bronx. Two generations in by 1925;
  the cop, the fireman, the dockworker.
- **German** — Yorkville, and older residue everywhere.
- **Black** — **Harlem, and by 1925 it is the great Black metropolis in America**, mid-Harlem
  around 135th Street, its own blocks with its own commerce and its own paper, the *Amsterdam
  News*. The same tenement block type, the same stickball, different faces on the stoop.
- **Puerto Rican** — arriving in East Harlem after 1917 and growing fast; by 1925 there is a
  real and visible Spanish-speaking presence pressed up against Italian Harlem, and *El Barrio*
  is forming. Correct for 1925, dominant only by the 1930s. Include it, don't over-weight it.
- **Chinese** — hand laundries scattered across every neighborhood, not just Chinatown.

**Practical direction:** a roster of eight to twelve kids should contain at least four of these
communities, they should be playing on the same team, and the game should be the thing they
have in common. That is not modern politesse — it is what the block was.

## 5.5 Superstitions, rituals, and how it feels to be nine here

- The stick gets **tapped on the manhole cover twice** before the batter sets. Everybody does it
  because the best hitter does it.
- The batter **spits in his palms and rubs them together**. Universal, imitated from ballplayers.
- **Rubbing the ball on your thigh** before pitching, because that is what you saw a man do.
- **Do not step on a crack** on the way to the plate. Real, widespread, and free animation.
- A found **horseshoe** nailed over a doorway; a **corno** (red coral horn) or a horseshoe over
  an Italian shop door; a red thread on an infant.
- **Bottle caps and cigarette cards.** Cards came in cigarette packs — kids collected, traded,
  and flipped them against a wall, and pitched pennies at a line in the sidewalk. Losing your
  cards is a plot.
- **The ownership hierarchy is emotional and absolute:** whoever owns the ball has power,
  whoever owns the stick has status, and whoever can hit two sewers is the mayor.
- Kids are unsupervised from three o'clock until the streetlights come on, and being called in
  early is a humiliation. The **lamplighter is gone by 1925** (electric lights) but the phrase
  "till the lights come on" is exactly the shape of the afternoon.

# 6. LIGHT AND WEATHER

## 6.1 The geometry of the light — actual numbers

New York City sits at **latitude 40.71° N, longitude 73.99° W**. Computed for **22 September
1925** (declination ≈ +0.4°, the day before the equinox), on **Eastern Daylight Time** — the city
was still on DST, which does not end until 27 September. Because declination is near zero the sun
rises very nearly due east and sets very nearly due west, which is what makes the arithmetic below
clean:

| Local time (EDT) | Sun altitude | Sun azimuth |
|---|---|---|
| **12:49** (solar noon) | **50°** | 180° (due south) |
| 2:50 pm | **41°** | 222° (SW) |
| 3:50 pm | **33°** | 237° |
| 4:50 pm | **22°** | 250° (WSW) |
| 5:50 pm | **11°** | 261° |
| **6:52 pm** | 0° — sunset | ~270° (due west) |

*(Corrected on this pass: solar noon is 12:49, not 12:53 — longitude puts local noon 56 minutes
after clock noon and the equation of time pulls ~7 minutes back off it — and the 2:50 figure was
a degree high. If you re-pin the date to 15 September the sun sits about 2° higher all afternoon
and sunset moves to ~7:03 pm; recompute, don't reuse.)*

**The Manhattan grid is rotated 29° east of north**, so a "cross street" actually runs on a
bearing of about 119°/299°. Working that against a **60 ft wide, 60 ft tall canyon** gives the
thing a builder actually needs — where the sunlight *is*:

- **Solar noon:** the shadow of the south row reaches ~44 ft across the 60 ft canyon, so there
  is a **~16 ft ribbon of direct sun along the north sidewalk** and nowhere else. Even at midday
  this street is mostly in shade.
- **~2:50 pm:** the shadow line has climbed to the base of the north facade. The **roadway is
  now entirely in shadow**; the north wall is lit from the sills up.
- **~4:50 pm — the money hour, and the hour to set the game in:** the roadway, both sidewalks,
  and every player are in **full shade**. The sunlight line sits about **27 ft up the north
  facade — right at the third-floor windowsills — and climbs steadily** through the rest of the
  afternoon toward the cornice. (Working: at 22° altitude and 41° off the facade normal, the ray
  travels 1.83 ft horizontally per foot it drops, so crossing 60 ft of street costs it 33 ft of
  height, and 60 − 33 = 27.)
- **~5:50 pm:** only the top floor and the cornice are lit. The cornices, the chimney pots, the
  water tanks, and the laundry on the top fire escapes are burning orange over a street that
  has gone entirely blue.

**This is the whole lighting design and it is not a stylistic choice — it is arithmetic.**
The players are in cool ambient shade. The upper facade is in warm direct sun. The eye goes to
the bright band and then drops to the action, which is exactly the composition you want.

## 6.2 Color of the light

- **Direct sun at 23° altitude, through a mile of coal-hazed air:** warm and thick.
  **`#FFC98A` → `#F5A863`** as it drops. Not white. Not lemon.
- **Skylight in the canyon (the key light on every character):** the players are lit by a strip
  of sky 60 ft wide seen from the bottom of a 60 ft trench, plus bounce off the sunlit north
  facade. That means **a soft cool overhead-and-frontal fill, `#8FA6C4` to `#A8B8CE`**, warmed
  from one side by orange brick bounce, **`#C08863` at maybe 20–30% of the fill**.
- **Bounce from the sunlit brick** is the secret weapon: the lit upper facade dumps a warm
  orange glow down onto the shaded street, so **the tops of heads, caps, and shoulders catch a
  faint warm rim** while the ground plane stays cool. That single relationship is what makes a
  street canyon read as a street canyon.
- **Shadows are not black and not neutral** — they are **blue-violet**, `#3E4658`, because the
  fill is sky. Terminator edges are soft; there is almost no hard shadow at street level.
- **Ambient occlusion is heavy and low**: under parked cars, under the stoops, in the areaways,
  under the cornices, under the fire escapes. The block is a stack of dark slots.

## 6.3 The air — coal haze

By the 1920s New York's air had gone bad: homes and businesses that could not get hard
anthracite burned **soft bituminous coal**, which smokes heavily. September is the start of the
heating season and the first fires of the year are being lit — and in **this** September the
anthracite fields have been on strike since the **1st**, with ~150,000 men out and no settlement
until February. Hard coal is scarce and the price is climbing. So the haze on this particular
block on this particular afternoon is not generic period smog; it is a specific, dated, arguable
economic fact, and the men on the stoop are talking about it.

- **Aerial perspective at very short range.** Distance fog should be readable **within 300 ft** —
  the far end of the block is measurably hazier than the near end. That is unusual and it is
  the look. Fog color **`#B9AE9E`**, warm gray-tan, tinted toward the sun.
- **Volumetric shafts** wherever sunlight gets between two buildings: a gap between cornices, a
  cross street at the far corner, the underside of the El. Dusty gold beams that you can see
  because the air has particulate in it.
- **Smoke sources:** a thin gray column from two or three chimney-pot clusters, kitchen smoke, a
  truck's exhaust, a man's cigarette, a burning-leaves-and-trash smell that has no visual but
  belongs in the design conversation.
- **Everything is filmed with soot.** Sills carry a dark line. Cornices are black on their
  upper faces. Window glass is grimy at the corners. Snow, if you ever build winter, is gray by
  the second day.

## 6.4 Set-piece lighting moments to build deliberately

1. **Backlit laundry on a top-floor fire escape** — a white sheet with sun behind it goes
   translucent and glows; you see the rope and the clothespins through the fabric as silhouettes.
2. **The sunlight band climbing the north facade** across the length of a game. If a match runs
   in real time, animate this. It is a clock and a mood in one.
3. **Pigeons crossing from shade into the sunlit band** — dark birds that flash white when they
   bank into the light. Free, and the most alive thing you can put in a frame.
4. **A shaft of sun down the cross street at the corner**, so a kid running to field a ball
   passes through one bar of gold and back into shade.
5. **Windows going on at dusk** — warm `#FFB460` rectangles appearing one at a time up the
   facade while the street goes blue. The end-of-game screen.
6. **Wet asphalt after a September shower** — a black mirror that doubles every sign and lamp,
   with the granite blocks showing through the worn patches darker than the asphalt.
7. **The El train's shadow strobing** across a wall at the end of the block.

## 6.5 Weather

Late September in New York: normals are a **high near 75–77 °F and a low near 60–62 °F**, so a
4:50 pm reading is within a couple of degrees of the day's high — call it **73–76 °F**, humid at
midday, cooling fast after five, and cold enough by full dark that the women on the stoop have
gone in for a sweater. Sky is a high pale blue **`#9FBBD6`** hazed to near-white at the horizon,
with the light broken cumulus of early autumn. A brief hard thundershower is plausible and clears in twenty minutes. Wind is
light — enough to stir laundry and lift a cap, not enough to move a tree, and there are no trees
on this block anyway. **Street trees on a 1925 tenement cross street: none, or one dying ailanthus
in a rear yard.** A leafy green residential street is the wrong city. `✗`

# 7. SOUND

The soundscape is layered, continuous, and made almost entirely of things that no longer exist.
Build it as **three beds plus events**.

**Bed 1 — the far city (continuous, low, filtered):**
- The **El**, a block and a half off: a rising steel roar with a rhythmic wheel-beat and a
  scream on the curve, 12–20 seconds, every 2–4 minutes, arriving before you see it and ringing
  in the structure after it has passed. Then the **descending whine of the brakes at the
  station** and the **clank of the gates**.
- Distant traffic hum, a mile of ferry and harbor whistles, and once in the mix a **deep river
  horn** that says the water is not far.

**Bed 2 — the block (continuous, mid, close):**
- Voices through open windows: a woman calling in Italian, a baby, an argument two floors up.
- A **radio in a third-floor window** — 1925 New York, so **WEAF** (AT&T's, 610 kc), **WJZ**
  (RCA's), **WHN**, or **WOR** from Newark, playing tinny and thin through a **horn speaker**
  with static on it. Not a console with a cloth grille — that is later; in 1925 a working-class
  set is a battery receiver with a separate brass or bakelite horn, and half the block is still
  listening on **headphones**, one earpiece each, which is a wonderful thing to stage in a window.
- Repertoire: dance-band jazz, *Yes! We Have No Bananas* (1923), *Charleston* (1923; the dance
  craze is peaking right now), *Rhapsody in Blue* (1924), *It Had to Be You* (1924),
  *Sweet Georgia Brown* (1925), *Five Foot Two, Eyes of Blue* (1925), *Manhattan* (Rodgers &
  Hart, June 1925), *Show Me the Way to Go Home* (1925), Paul Whiteman's orchestra.
  **Anything after September 1925 is wrong** — the previous draft said "after 1926," which is a
  year of slack this scene cannot afford. No swing, no big band, no crooner, no electrically
  recorded warmth: electrical recording only arrives in 1925 and what is coming out of that
  window is thin, mid-heavy and acoustic.
- A **sewing machine** treadling, a piano being practiced badly, someone beating a rug.
- Pigeon wings and pigeon murmur from the roof; sparrows in the gutter.

**Bed 3 — the ground (continuous, textural):**
- Shoe leather on grit. A ball hitting asphalt. The specific hollow **"tonk"** of a broom handle
  on a hollow rubber ball — dry, light, high, and nothing like a bat on a baseball.
- Iron: the **fire escape ringing** when a kid climbs; the **hollow boom of a manhole cover**
  when someone steps on it wrong; the clatter of an ash can lid.

**Events (fire these on triggers):**
- **Klaxon "ah-OO-gah"** — the electric klaxon horn is *the* 1920s car horn: a two-tone rasping
  bray, not a modern beep, not a bulb-horn "honk." One rasping klaxon is worth more period than
  any texture on screen. (By the 1930s these are gone, which is why they date a scene so hard.)
- **Model T engine:** a light, fast, uneven four-cylinder clatter with a distinctive
  **planetary-transmission whine** and a rattling body. It is *loud* and *tinny*.
- **Iron-tired wheels and shod hooves on granite block** — a hard, ringing, unmistakable clop
  and rumble. On asphalt the same horse is soft and dull. Use the difference.
- **Knife grinder** working the block: a **hand bell** and a call — *"Scissors! Knives to
  grind!"* — then the **shriek of a blade on a foot-treadle wheel** and a fan of sparks.
- **Ragman / old-clothes peddler:** a long two-note call — *"I cash clothes!"* — worn down over
  fifty years to something barely verbal.
- **Fruit peddler** chanting prices off a cart, half in Italian, half in English, on a melody.
- **The iceman:** *"ICE! I-I-ICE!"*, the ice saw, the tongs biting, and water on the asphalt.
- **Church bells** — the Angelus at six, a full peal on a saint's day, from two different
  churches at slightly different pitches and not quite in time with each other.
- **A dog** on a rope in an areaway, barking at everything, and one specific bark for the
  iceman's horse.
- **A mother out a window:** the two-syllable name held long and dropping — *"FRAAAAN-kieeee!"*
  This is the most emotionally loaded sound in the whole design. It ends games.
- **A cop's whistle** — a shrill metal pea-whistle, two short blasts, and the game evaporates.
- **A fire engine** — a hand-cranked or motor siren and a **bell**, and every kid on the block
  runs toward it.

**Sound anti-patterns:** no rubber-tire screech (tires are hard and speeds are 20 mph), no
modern siren wail, no car alarms, no jackhammer, no jet or prop aircraft overhead, no
air-conditioner hum, no cheerful "old timey ragtime piano" wall-to-wall — the block is not a
saloon and the music comes from *one window*, faintly, badly.

# 8. THE AUTHENTICITY CHECKLIST

A frame that carries most of these reads as 1925 New York. Count them. **Anything below 25 out
of 40 in a hero shot is a fail.** Items 31–40 were added on the verification pass: they are the
things that were missing from the first draft's checklist and are, in practice, what separates a
careful build from a good one.

1. **Broom handle, not a bat** — thin, straight, taped grip, no barrel, no knob.
2. **A stripped tennis ball or a plain unbranded rubber ball**, dirty, dented, ~2-1/4 in, with
   the tennis seam still readable — visible as pink against gray asphalt. **Not** a stamped
   Spalding High-Bounce ball (§1.2).
3. **A manhole cover as home plate**, with real cast pattern and real letters on it.
4. **Chalk marks** on the asphalt and a chalk scorecard on the curb, both half scuffed away.
5. **Bare hands. No gloves anywhere in frame.**
6. **A 1:1 street canyon** — 60 ft wide, 60 ft of building on both sides, continuous, no gaps.
7. **A cornice line that varies** building to building in height, color, and bracket count.
8. **Projecting lintels over every window**, casting a rhythm of little hard shadows.
9. **Fire escapes on the street facade**, zig-zagged, see-through, projecting no more than
   4 ft 6 in from the building line, with the drop ladder at rest **at least 10 ft** above the
   sidewalk — out of a kid's reach, by statute.
10. **Stoops projecting into the sidewalk** with people sitting on them.
11. **A leaning figure in an upper window**, elbows on a folded towel.
12. **Laundry on a fire escape**, and none strung across the street.
13. **Roofline of chimney pots**, at least one water tank on a taller building behind, and
    a pigeon coop.
14. **Awnings** — canvas, striped, cranked down on the sunny side, scalloped valance.
15. **Hand-lettered signage on every available surface**, all-caps, shaded, in five faces.
16. **At least one ghost sign** — a faded painted brick ad with an older one showing through.
17. **Gold-leaf lettering** on at least one shop window.
18. **A bishop's-crook lamppost** at the curb, fluted, with a garland below the crook.
19. **A Johnny pump** used as a base, painted and chipped.
20. **An olive-drab mailbox** and a **red Gamewell fire alarm box on a fluted pedestal**.
21. **Black Model Ts, taller than they are wide and only ~10 ft 8 in long**, on wood spokes and
    skinny clinchers, parked badly and used as bases.
22. **A horse still working** — ice, coal, milk, or produce — in the same frame as a car.
23. **A pushcart** with iron-tired wheels and a hanging scale.
24. **Granite curbs and bluestone flags**, plus Belgian block surfacing through worn asphalt.
25. **Ash cans** at the curb, galvanized, dented, full of coal ash.
26. **Newsboy caps, knickers buckled at the knee, sagging long stockings, ankle boots**, and
    everyone's clothes a size wrong.
27. **Everyone over sixteen wearing a hat**, and men in shirtsleeves-and-vest, not jackets.
28. **Coal haze** — visible aerial perspective within 300 feet, warm gray-tan.
29. **The sunlight band high on the facade, the street in blue shade**, per §6.1.
30. **Something moving that nobody is controlling**: pigeons, laundry, smoke, a horse's tail, a
    curtain, a woman shaking a rug out a window, an El train at the end of the street.

31. **An unbroken wall of building** — party wall to party wall for the whole 200 ft, with no
    gap, no driveway, no side yard, no setback, and no daylight between any two buildings.
32. **Vault lights** in the sidewalk — the cast-iron grid of round glass prisms, sun-purpled to
    amethyst and worn smooth.
33. **Sidewalk cellar doors**, two steel leaves flush in the walk, diamond treadplate, chained
    through a hasp, rust-brown — and somebody standing on them.
34. **A recessed storefront entry** with a hexagonal white tile floor and the shop's initial laid
    in black tile.
35. **Goods spilling 3–4 ft onto the sidewalk** from every store that has a door — crates,
    barrels, a broom rack, a stand of papers. Stores in 1925 do not stay inside their walls.
36. **A price in cents with a real ¢ sign**, hand-lettered, and it is the second-biggest thing on
    the panel after the shop's name.
37. **Torn paper on a wall that can be dated to the week** — a film bill and a political bill,
    pasted over each other, one fresh and one sun-bleached (§4.5).
38. **A telephone number as an exchange name plus four digits** on the side of a truck —
    `HARLEM 4417` — because nothing else on the block is that cheap and that unfakeable.
39. **Something already broken and not fixed** — a cracked sill, a split awning, a boarded pane,
    a stoop tread worn into a dish, a downspout missing its bottom four feet.
40. **A dead spot.** One stretch of curb with nothing on it, one blank party wall, one unlit
    window. A block that is interesting at every point is a diorama, not a street.

**And the three that are worth ten each:**
- The **"CAR!"** interrupt, played as a full beat.
- The **hand-over-hand up the broom handle** to pick teams.
- A **two-sewer shot** measured out loud, in sewers, by the kids.

# 9. THE ANTI-CHECKLIST — refuse these

**Wrong decade / wrong object**
1. `✗` **A Model A Ford, or any 1930s car.** The Model A is December 1927. Its 1928 colors
   (Niagara Blue, Arabian Sand, Thorne Brown) are three years away in September 1925.
2. `✗` **Colored Model Ts in quantity.** 1914–1925 Model Ts are black. One repainted oddball, fine.
3. `✗` **A red, white, and blue or blue mailbox.** Olive drab until 1955.
4. `✗` **Aluminum bats. Batting helmets. Baseball gloves. Bases. A pitcher's mound. Foul poles.
    A backstop. A scoreboard.** None of it. It is a stick, a ball, and a manhole cover.
5. `✗` **A basketball hoop on the fire escape or over a garage door.** Wrong sport, wrong decade,
    wrong architecture — there are no garages on this block.
6. `✗` **A chain-link fence.** Reads instantly as post-war. Use iron railings, wooden hoarding
    with posters pasted on it, or a brick party wall.
7. `✗` **Plastic anything.** No traffic cones, no bins, no crates, no bottles, no toys.
    Wood, iron, canvas, tin, glass, paper, rubber, leather. That is the entire material list.
8. `✗` **Modern road markings** — no painted center line, no crosswalk zebra, no stop line, no
    yellow curb paint, no parking signs, no traffic signals mid-block. Traffic control in 1925
    is a cop in the intersection and a hand-cranked semaphore tower on the big avenues only.
9. `✗` **A t-shirt, a hoodie, a baseball cap with a curved brim, sneakers with a swoosh,
    jeans on a child.** Also no printed graphics on any garment, ever.
10. `✗` **Rooftop TV antennas, satellite dishes, air conditioners, roll-down security gates,
    parking meters (1935), neon in a residential block, or fluorescent light.**
11. `✗` **Overhead trolley wire on a Manhattan street.** Manhattan used an underground conduit.
    (Overhead wire is correct in Brooklyn and much of the Bronx — know which one you built.)
12. `✗` **Telephone poles and overhead utility wires on a Manhattan block.** Buried since the 1890s.
13. `✗` **A visible bar, tavern, beer sign, or liquor store.** It is Prohibition. Paint it out
    and let the ghost bleed through.
14. `✗` **"Willie Mays was a four-sewer man"** in 1925 dialogue. Right sport, wrong decade.
15. `✗` **The Spalding High-Bounce ball itself, not merely the word.** The stamped pink Spalding
    is documented as a **1930s** product, popular 1930s–70s; "Spaldeen" as a word is later still.
    In 1925 the ball is a **stripped tennis ball or a plain unbranded rubber ball** (§1.2). This
    is the single likeliest anachronism in the whole build, because it is the thing every
    reference photograph of stickball will hand you — and almost every one of those photographs
    is from **1947–1960**.

**Wrong texture / wrong feeling**
16. `✗` **A clean street.** No sanitized asphalt, no fresh paint, no crisp mortar, no unbroken
    glass. Equally: no *set-dressed squalor*. It is a working block that is swept every morning
    and dirty by noon.
17. `✗` **Rounded river cobblestones.** By 1925 it is asphalt over tooled Belgian block.
18. `✗` **A laundry-line cat's cradle strung across the street above the game.** Cinematic lie;
    laundry goes to the rear yard and onto fire escapes.
19. `✗` **Uniform buildings.** Identical extruded facades at identical heights with identical
    cornices is the number-one tell of a procedural block. Vary height by 2–8 ft, brick color,
    cornice paint, window sash color, and fire escape rust per building.
20. `✗` **A wide, bright, airy street.** If sunlight is falling on the pavement at 4 pm the
    canyon is too wide or the buildings are too short.
21. `✗` **Green leafy street trees**, lawns, front gardens, driveways, or a detached house.
22. `✗` **Empty sidewalks.** The buildings hold 80–100 people each. The street is never empty.
23. `✗` **Hollywood gangster kitsch** — pinstripe suits, fedoras pulled low, tommy-gun
    violin cases, a black sedan idling with a man on the running board, anyone called "Bugsy."
    This is a residential block on a Tuesday afternoon. The men in frame are icemen and
    shopkeepers.
24. `✗` **Sepia.** Do not desaturate the world to signal "old." 1925 was in color and this
    block is full of it: painted signs, canvas awnings, produce, gold leaf, red brick,
    green lampposts. Color is period-truthful; sepia is a photographic artifact of the medium.
25. `✗` **Generic "old timey" mush** — a barrel, a wagon wheel, a hitching rail, and a
    swinging-door saloon. That is the American frontier West, not New York, and it is 60 years
    and 2,000 miles off.
26. `✗` **Ragtime piano playing everywhere.** One radio, one window, thin and far.
27. `✗` **Cars parked in neat modern parallel rows at even spacing.** They are parked badly, at
    angles, with gaps, some facing the wrong way.
28. `✗` **Adults supervising the game.** Nobody is coaching. Nobody is watching except from a
    stoop, and they are mostly watching each other.

**Wrong by a few years — the expensive mistakes** *(added on the verification pass; these are the
ones that survive a casual look and fail a specialist's)*

29. `✗` **`THE BIG PARADE` or `BEN-HUR` on a poster.** The two most famous films of 1925 both
    opened *after* September: 5/19 November and 30 December respectively. A release *year* is not
    a date. Use `THE GOLD RUSH` (New York from 16 August) and `THE FRESHMAN` (opened 20 September,
    two days old) — §4.5.
30. `✗` **A Feast of San Gennaro banner.** The Mulberry Street feast was **first held in 1926**.
    East Harlem's feast is Our Lady of Mount Carmel and it is in **July**.
31. `✗` **A zipper, anywhere.** B. F. Goodrich coined the name "Zipper" in **1923** for rubber
    galoshes; clothing fasteners follow at the end of the decade and zippers only beat buttons for
    the trouser fly in **1937**. In 1925 it is buttons, hooks, laces, pins, and string.
32. `✗` **A self-service grocery, a shopping cart, a checkout lane.** The first supermarket —
    King Kullen — opens in Jamaica, Queens, in **August 1930**. The 1925 A&P is a small
    counter-service store where a clerk in an apron fetches your order off a shelf you cannot
    reach, weighs it, and writes the total on a paper bag.
33. `✗` **"DEPARTMENT OF SANITATION" or "DSNY"** on a cart, a can, a uniform, or a badge. In 1925
    it is the **Department of Street Cleaning**; DSNY is **1929**.
34. `✗` **The wrong skyline.** No **Chrysler Building** (1930), **Empire State** (1931), **40
    Wall Street** (1930), **George Washington Bridge** (1931) or **Triborough** (1936). In
    September 1925 the tallest building in the world is still the **Woolworth Building** (1913,
    792 ft) — and from a tenement cross street you cannot see it anyway, which is the real answer:
    the view down the block ends in the El, not in a skyline.
35. `✗` **An IND subway entrance or an IND station tile.** The IND opens in **1932**. The only
    subway in 1925 is IRT and BMT, and up here it is mostly elevated anyway.
36. `✗` **"Now you're cooking."** 1930s, out of gas-industry advertising. Cut from §1.9 on this
    pass.
37. `✗` **A domed custodian helmet on the cop.** Peaked cap by 1925 (§5.2). And he is walking.
38. `✗` **Wire wheels as the default on a Model T**, and any Model T longer than about 10 ft 8 in.
    Wood artillery spokes, skinny clinchers, short and tall (§3.8).
39. `✗` **Organized, uniformed stickball** — matching shirts, a sponsor's name across the chest,
    a trophy, an adult in a cap keeping score. Leagues, uniforms and citywide tournaments are the
    **post-1945** stickball revival. In 1925 the only uniform on the block is what your mother
    put you in that morning.
40. `✗` **A pizza slice, a slice counter, or a window with a round pie in it.** Whole-pie
    pizzerias exist in New York from 1905, so a `PIZZERIA` sign is arguable in an Italian
    neighborhood — but the by-the-slice trade, the sidewalk window and the folded slice in a
    kid's hand are post-war. `~`
41. `✗` **Eight buildings claimed to run from one avenue to the next.** The 200-ft/8-lot figure is
    the block's *depth*; a cross-street blockface is **600–920 ft** (§2.1). Build 8 lots as a
    mid-block stretch and keep both corners out of shot or far away.
42. `✗` **Sewers spaced at one distance in the geometry and quoted at another in the dialogue.**
    Pick 95 ft, hard-code it, and make every boast in the game consistent with it (§1.3).
43. `✗` **Standard time.** The city is on **daylight saving** until 27 September 1925. An hour's
    error at 4 pm in September is 11° of sun altitude — an entirely different street.

## 10.3 Confidence notes

- **Solid:** lot dimensions and street widths; the 1811 grid's 200 ft × 600–920 ft blocks; the
  1885 height act (70/80 ft); tenement massing and the dumbbell plan (14 rooms per floor);
  fire-escape statute (MDL §53 — 10 ft minimum clearance, 4 ft 6 in maximum projection, 80 psf);
  mailbox colour chronology (1909 dark green → olive drab → 4 July 1955); the Gamewell
  instruction text; "Johnny pump"; Manhattan conduit trolleys and the ban on overhead wire;
  Model T colour policy and dimensions; paving history and the 140 miles of Belgian block still
  down in 1949; water-tank rules; documented manhole foundries; the 1920s brand slogans; boys'
  clothing; the neighbourhood mix; coal haze and the September 1925 anthracite strike; the sun
  geometry in §6.1; the September 1925 news, film and election dates; New York's 1925 daylight
  saving dates.
- **Reconstructed but defensible (`~`):** exact 1925 stickball vernacular (the game is
  documented, the transcripts are not); "Hindu" as a do-over call; the specific fire-escape
  ground rules (they varied block to block and were argued about, which is the point); the
  precise hex values; the fast-pitch strike-zone dimensions; air-shaft width; sewer spacing
  (real spacing varied from ~100 ft in old districts to 150–160 ft elsewhere — 95 ft is the
  stickball convention, not a survey); the bishop's-crook luminaire height above the 13 ft of
  documented cast iron; the White Wing's white uniform surviving to September 1925; pink as the
  common colour of a 1925 street rubber ball.
- **Demoted on the verification pass — was stated as certain, is not:**
  the **pink Spalding High-Bounce ball** (1930s product, moved to `✗` for a stamped ball and `~`
  for a generic pink one); the **200-ft blockface** (was a straight factual error — it is the
  block's depth); **`THE BIG PARADE` and `BEN-HUR`** posters (both post-date the scene);
  **`FESTA DI SAN GENNARO`** (first held 1926); **"Now yer cookin'"** (1930s); the **cop's domed
  helmet** (gone by the 1900s); **4 brick courses per foot** (it is ~4.5); the **8–12 ft drop
  ladder** (statutory minimum is 10 ft); the **12–14 ft bishop's crook** (its cast-iron shaft
  alone is 13 ft); **`ABBOTT HARDWARE CO.`** on a manhole cover (unverifiable, cut);
  **solar noon at 12:53** (it is 12:49).
- **Deliberately excluded:** anything after **September 1925**, anything from the Depression-era
  stickball boom, and anything from the 1940s–50s Spaldeen era that the internet will hand you
  first when you search "stickball." Most stickball imagery online is **1947–1960**. It is the
  wrong decade, it will pull the whole build twenty years late, and — as §1.2 shows — it already
  did once in the first draft of this document.
