# BYB-REFERENCE — Backyard Baseball as a Measurable Standard

The canonical reference for every builder and every critic on this project.
Subject: **Humongous Entertainment's *Backyard Baseball* (1997)**, with *Backyard Baseball 2001*
and the wider *Backyard Sports* line as corroborating evidence.

This is not a nostalgia document. It exists so that a critic holding a rendered PNG of our
1920s stickball game can put a number or a yes/no next to a claim. If a line in here cannot be
checked against a screenshot or a frame burst, it does not belong in here — tell the maintainer.

---

## 0. How to read this file (provenance and confidence)

Three kinds of statement appear below. Do not confuse them.

* **[FACT]** — documented in the game, its files, its press, or developer interviews. Cite-able,
  and **always tagged with the game and year it belongs to**, e.g. **[FACT, 1997]**.
  Example: the pitcher's stamina meter is drawn as a **juice box**.
* **[OBSERVED]** — consistently reported by players, reviewers and archivists describing what is
  on screen. Reliable, but described in prose rather than measured.
* **[TARGET]** — a number *this project* commits to, derived from the observed design language
  and expressed so a critic can measure it in a PNG. These are our operational definitions of
  "Backyard-grade". A builder who misses a TARGET has not necessarily contradicted the 1997 game
  — they have failed *our* bar, which is the only thing that matters here.

Where a number appears without a tag, treat it as **[TARGET]**.

**Every [FACT] must name the game and the year it comes from.** The series runs 1997 to 2026 and
features move between entries: the juice-box stamina meter is 1997; the orange/green/blue "good
day" icon is *Backyard Baseball 2001*; Pablo's English reveal is *2003*; the fireworks home run
and the Griddy celebration are the *2026* game, not the 2024 remaster of '97. An unattributed
[FACT] in this file is a bug — report it. A claim repeated across fan wikis with no primary source
is tagged **[OBSERVED]**, never [FACT].

A note on the era gap: Backyard Baseball is a suburban American sandlot in 1997. We are building
a **1920s New York City stickball game**. Section 10 is the translation table. Everywhere else,
read "backyard" as "the block", "fence" as "stoop / iron rail / tenement wall", and "sky over the
lawn" as "sky over the rooftops". The *design language* transfers wholesale. The props do not.

## 1. Character design language

### 1.1 The core proportion contract

* **How to measure it, so two critics get the same number.** *Head height* = crown of the skull to
  the bottom of the chin, **excluding hair volume, cap and brim**. *Standing height* = crown (again
  excluding hair and hat) to the sole of the shoe, on a kid standing upright. Report the ratio to
  one decimal place, and say which kid you measured.
* Kids stand **3.0 to 3.5 head-heights tall**. The head occupies **29–33% of standing height**.
  Anything at 4.5+ heads reads as a small adult and fails. Anything at 2.5 or below reads as a
  bobblehead toy and also fails — Backyard kids are *kids*, not chibi mascots.
* **[TARGET] The roster must spread across that band, not cluster inside it.** Across nine fielders
  the head-height ratios must span at least **1.0 head-height** end to end (e.g. 2.8 up to 3.9),
  with no more than **three** kids inside any 0.2-wide bucket. A roster where everyone measures 3.2
  is a recolour roster with extra steps.
* **[FACT, 1997]** The head-size joke is inside the game itself: **"Melonheads" is one of the ten
  stock team names** (alongside All-Stars, Bombers, Fishes, Giants, Hornets, Monsters, Rockets,
  Socks and Wombats), and the adjective+noun name builder lets a player assemble "Humongous
  Melonheads" on purpose. The developers knew the head was the read, and let the player say so.
* Torso is short and boxy — roughly **1 head-height** from shoulder line to hip. Legs are
  **1.2–1.5 head-heights**. Arms hang to mid-thigh, not to the knee.
* Shoulders are **narrower than the head is wide**, or at most equal. This is the single most
  reliable "child" cue in the whole style. The moment shoulders exceed head width, the silhouette
  becomes an adult athlete and the joy drains out.
* **Necks are near-zero.** Head sits on shoulders with 0–0.15 head-heights of neck. A visible neck
  column is an adult cue.
* Hands are **mitts, not anatomy**: rounded four-finger or fused-mitten forms, width **55–70% of
  head width**. Fingers, if separated at all, are separated by notches, not by joints.
* Feet/shoes are deliberately oversized: shoe **length ≈ 0.7–0.9 head-heights**. **[OBSERVED]** The
  series' own design language is described as "cartoony big hands and feet to make animations look
  and feel exaggerated." Big extremities are load-bearing — they are what makes a run cycle funny.
* **Deviation from the base proportion is how you make a character.** The roster does not use one
  rig with recolours:
  * **Pablo "The Secret Weapon" Sanchez** — short and wide, ~2.8 heads, bald, tan-skinned,
    pot-bellied. **[FACT, 1997]** the shirt rides up and the navel shows — The Ringer's
    20th-anniversary feature calls him "the rotund, belly button-baring 'secret weapon'". He sits
    deliberately below the 3.0 floor: he is the short end of the spread, and the exception is what
    proves the band.
  * **Ricky Johnson** — **[FACT, 1997]** an **oval-shaped bald head**, a baggy white tee, baggy
    jeans, white sneakers; one of the tallest kids at ~4 heads. "Tall kid" is the first read from
    silhouette; the bald oval is the second.
  * **Pete Wheeler** — **[FACT, 1997]** an *abnormally tall head*, orange hair, freckles, a white
    pullover, the **right** eyelid droopier than the left, tongue frequently hanging out of his
    mouth. Comedy is built into the skull shape, and the asymmetry is on a specific, named side.
  * **Billy Jean "Crazy Legs" Blackwood** — **[FACT, 1997]** the tallest kid in the league, a
    lanky Southern belle with middling stats. **[OBSERVED]** ponytail, trousers stopping above the
    ankle. Height *and* hemline: two cues, one read.
  * **Ernie "Funnybones" / "Big Easy" Steele** — **[FACT, 1997]** the roster's jokester, known
    for his height, and the owner of *Steele Stadium*, which is literally his own backyard. Two
    nicknames, one of them about his skeleton: joke and silhouette agree.
  * **Kenny "K-Man" Kawaguchi** — **[FACT, 1997]** the only wheelchair user: **blue** chair,
    **red** shirt, **blue** baseball cap. His silhouette is a *different shape entirely* from every
    other kid on the field, and he is an elite pitcher carrying only a 2 in batting. The chair
    changes the outline, never the competence.
  * **Marky Dubois** — **[FACT, 1997]** wears **overalls and nothing else**, plays **barefoot**,
    talks with a Southern drawl, and **carries a live frog in his front pocket everywhere he goes,
    including into the batter's box**. **[OBSERVED]** bowl cut. Two props — no shoes, one frog —
    carry a whole backstory, and the frog is the better of the two because it can *move*.
  * **Dmitri Ivan "Paste" Petrovich** — **[FACT, 1997]** large, nerdy, glasses, obsessed with
    technology, and he **eats paste**, which is where the nickname comes from. The gag is a habit,
    not a garment — habits animate, garments do not.
  * **Vicki "Swan" Kawaguchi** — **[FACT, 1997]** plays sports in a **pink tutu**. Nickname and
    costume are the same joke, which is exactly why both stick.
  * **Maria Luna** — **[FACT, 1997]** long dark-brown-to-black hair in pigtails, **pink ribbons**,
    a **frilly pink dress**, **black Mary Janes**, brown skin. Playing baseball in a party dress is
    the joke and the character.
  * **Tony Delvecchio** — **[FACT, 1997]** an Italian-American greaser caricature: dark brown hair
    slicked into a greasy **pompadour with an excessive bulb of hair above the forehead**.
  * **Kimmy Eckman** — **[FACT, 1997]** small, red hair in pigtails with **bows tied at the ends**;
    owner of *Eckman Acres*. **[FACT, later games]** braces and a barrette are added in subsequent
    entries — proof that a roster can gain a silhouette hook between releases, and that ours may.
  * **Achmed "Axeman" Khan** — **[FACT, 1997]** tanned skin, curly dark-brown hair, a yellow
    shirt and **red headphones**; his theme music is an homage to Van Halen's "Jump", and he
    **air-guitars with the bat on the way to the plate**. Prop, music and animation all say the
    same word about him. **[OBSERVED]** his brother **Amir** is a shorter near-copy without the
    headphones — the game reuses a design on purpose and the difference survives blackout.
  * The rest of the thirty are built the same way, shape plus behaviour:
    **Gretchen "Jabberjaw" Hasselhoff** (the hyperactive overtalker — fast, weak throwing arm),
    **Kiesha Phillips** (elite power *and* speed, the cleanup bat),
    **Reese Worthington** (small, frail, blond, asthmatic, a show-off),
    **Angela Delvecchio** (Tony's sister, "two left feet" — strong pitching, subpar running,
    poor fielding), **Stephanie Morgan** (so baseball-obsessed she turns up at *football* tryouts),
    **Ashley and Sidney Webber** (identical twins who *share* one nickname, "Little Smokey").
    **[TARGET]** every kid on our roster must be describable in **one clause naming a shape and one
    clause naming a behaviour**, exactly as above. If the only clause available is a colour, that
    kid is not designed yet.

### 1.2 The silhouette test

Fill every kid on screen with solid black. **[TARGET]** At 96px tall you must still be able to
name at least **6 of 9 fielders**. If you cannot, the roster is recolours, not characters.

The hooks that survive blackout, ranked by how well they work:
1. **Head shape** (bald oval, tall dome, football, melon).
2. **Hair mass** (pigtails, ponytail, pompadour bulb, bowl cut, afro puff, cap).
3. **Attached prop** that breaks the outline (headphones, wheelchair, tutu hem, glasses arms,
   a bat held wrong, a cap worn backwards).
4. **Height and width class** — short-and-wide, tall-and-lanky, average.
5. Clothing hem lines (skirt, overalls, high-water trousers, bare feet).

Colour is **not on that list at all**. It is the sixth hook, and the blackout test is precisely the
test that deletes it. A roster legible only in colour fails, and it fails silently until somebody
thresholds the image. **[TARGET]** run the blackout for real: `node tools/shoot.mjs --out shots/x`,
open the fielding frame, threshold it so every kid is solid black on white, then write down how
many of the nine you can name. That number goes in the review.

### 1.3 Faces

* **Eyes:** large, wide-set, whites visible, pupils rendered as solid dark ovals or dots.
  **[TARGET]** eye width ≈ **12–16% of head width** each; the gap between them ≈ **one eye width**.
  Eyes sit **at or slightly below the horizontal midline of the head** — high foreheads read as
  adult, low eyes read as baby.
* **Eyebrows are separate floating shapes** above the eye — never welded to a brow ridge, never
  skin-coloured. **[TARGET]** brow length ≈ **1.0–1.4× eye width**; brow-to-eye gap sweeps
  **0.15–0.6 eye-heights** across the expression set; the rig reaches at least **four** named
  positions (level, both-raised, angry-inward, one-up-one-down). Test: crop a 64px-tall head to the
  brows alone, cover the mouth, and ask a critic to name the mood. If they can't, the brows are
  decoration and the face is carried entirely by the mouth.
* **Mouth vocabulary — six shapes, no more.** Any expression on any kid should reduce to one of:
  1. flat line (neutral / concentrating)
  2. open lower-arc grin (happy, teeth optional)
  3. open circle/oval (shout, surprise, "aaah")
  4. wide toothy grin with visible upper teeth (taunt, cocky)
  5. downturned arc (dejection after a strikeout)
  6. gritted-teeth zigzag (effort — the swing, the sprint)
  **[TARGET]** if a critic cannot classify every visible mouth in a frame into one of these six,
  the face rig is too naturalistic.
* **Asymmetry is character, not error.** Pete Wheeler's mismatched eyelids are the model. One
  raised brow, one closed eye, a tongue out of one corner — build these in deliberately.
* Noses are minimal: a bump, a dot, a comma. Ears are optional and frequently absent under hair.

### 1.4 "Kid athlete, not pro athlete"

This is the design ethic in a sentence, and it is checkable:

* **No muscle definition. Anywhere.** No deltoid, no calf, no forearm taper. Limbs are tubes.
* **Ill-fitting, non-uniform clothing.** Shirts untucked, one sock down, jeans instead of pants,
  a dress, a tutu, overalls with no shirt under them. **[FACT, 1997 vs Soccer '98]** the rule is
  exact and documented: in *Baseball '97* the kids wear **their own clothes with only a
  team-coloured cap**; from *Backyard Soccer '98* onward they wear a **team-coloured jersey over**
  their own clothes. 1997 is the un-kitted end of that pair, and it is the end we copy.
  **[TARGET]** our team marker is **one** period accessory — a cap band, an armband, a chalked
  number — covering **≤ 15% of a kid's silhouette area**. If a stranger can tell which team a kid
  is on before they can tell *which kid it is*, we have built a kit and lost the roster.
* **Equipment is mismatched and slightly too big.** Gloves that swallow a forearm; a batting
  helmet that sits low; a bat visibly heavy for the kid holding it.
* **Bad form, held with total confidence.** Stances are wrong — feet too wide, bat too vertical,
  elbow dropped — and the kid does not know it. **[FACT, 1997]** Pablo's compressed stance was
  drawn from **Edgar Martínez's**; the developers caricatured *real* mechanics down to a child's
  clumsy version rather than inventing generic poses. **[TARGET]** every one of our batting stances
  must be traceable to a named real stance — period newsreel, a photograph, a specific player — and
  the brief must say which. "A generic batting pose" is not a stance.
* **[FACT, Backyard Baseball 2001]** When the MLBPA licence let Humongous ship kid versions of real
  pros, artist **Tom Verre** described the job as "taking something that was identifiable about the
  major league players and just bringing it into that Backyard Baseball style": the **gap in Ken
  Griffey Jr.'s front teeth**, **Randy Johnson's beanstalk legs**, and — the one that matters most
  to us — **Nomar Garciaparra's long pre-pitch routine**, a caricature that is a *behaviour*
  rather than a shape. Verre also drew Jeter as a plausible real kid and **nobody recognised him**;
  the pros who lacked a distinctive trait (his example is Shawn Green) were the hard ones.
  **The lesson: caricature the identity, not the age — and a tic counts as a trait.**
* Roster demographics are part of the craft. **[FACT, 1997]** the game shipped **30 named kids —
  15 boys and 15 girls** — with a roughly even white / non-white split, and the wheelchair user is
  an *elite pitcher*. Nobody is a token and nobody is the mascot of their trait.
  **[TARGET]** our roster is **30 kids, 15/15**, and the best hitter and the best pitcher are two
  different kids.

## 2. Colour and light

### 2.1 Palette temperature and saturation

* **Warm-dominant, high-saturation, low-value-range.** The frame is built out of mid-to-light
  values; true black appears only in linework and pupils, and true white only in sky highlights,
  the ball, and eye whites.
* **[TARGET] Saturation floor:** the three largest colour areas in the frame (ground, sky,
  back wall) should sit at **S ≥ 0.35** in HSL. Desaturated "realistic" dirt and concrete are the
  fastest way to lose the look.
* **[TARGET] Hue count:** a typical gameplay frame carries **7–12 distinct hues**. *Distinct* means
  **≥ 20° apart in HSL hue** and occupying **≥ 1% of frame area** — that definition is the whole
  check, so use it rather than eyeballing. Count by sampling the ground, the sky, the
  back-wall/structure, the foliage, then one hue per kid's shirt. **Hard fail below 6 or above 15.**
  Under 6 reads as a tech demo; over 15 reads as noise.
* **Every kid is a colour accent.** Shirts are pure, named colours — yellow, orange, red, fuchsia,
  pink, blue, green — at high saturation, each roughly **1–3% of frame area**. Nine such accents
  scattered across a low-saturation-variance field is what makes the frame sing.
* **[TARGET] No two adjacent fielders share a shirt hue.** Enforce it at team-assembly time.

### 2.2 Field against sky

* The ground plane is **warmer and darker** than the sky; the sky is **cooler and lighter**.
  **[TARGET]** measured as **CIE L\***, the median L\* of the largest sky region must exceed the
  median L\* of the largest ground region by **15–30 points**, and never by less than 10. Sample a
  32×32 px patch at the centre of each region rather than a single pixel. Sky hue must also be
  **cooler**: sky hue angle in the 190–250° band, ground hue in the 20–60° band. When the two
  converge, the horizon dies and the frame flattens.
* Skies are not flat blue. **[TARGET]** a vertical gradient of at least **two stops**, plus at
  least **one non-sky element** in the upper third — a cloud, a rooftop, a wire, a bird, a kite,
  laundry, smoke. An empty upper third is the most common failure in an unfinished frame.
* **[FACT, 1997]** Surface material is a gameplay-visible variable and the palette follows it:
  *Sandy Flats* is pale sand; *Cement Gardens* is cement and *Tin Can Alley* is asphalt;
  *Dirt Yards* is brown dirt; *Steele Stadium* and *Eckman Acres* are green lawn; the *Super
  Colossal Dome* is AstroTurf. The ground hue must tell you which venue you are in **before you
  read a single word of text**. **[TARGET]** our five surfaces (cobblestone, asphalt, packed dirt,
  swept cement, a patch of weeds) must be separable by ground hue alone in a 64×64 px crop.

### 2.3 Shadow treatment

* **Contact shadows are mandatory and they are the cheapest joy in the frame.** Every kid, every
  base, every prop gets a soft dark ellipse under it.
  **[TARGET]** shadow opacity **0.25–0.45**; blur radius **8–14% of the ellipse's long axis**;
  ellipse long axis **0.9–1.2× the subject's shoulder width** for a standing kid, shrinking with
  height off the ground; tinted toward the *complement* of the ground hue rather than pure grey —
  warm dirt takes a cool-violet shadow. A shadow that is pure `#000` at any opacity is wrong.
* Shadows are **contact-only**, not cast-direction-accurate. Nobody is checking the sun angle.
  What they *are* checking is whether the kids are standing on the ground or floating above it.
  A missing contact shadow is an instant fail on the Craft axis.
* **[FACT, 1997]** The **ball's ground shadow is a gameplay mechanic**, not decoration — you field
  by moving a kid onto the marker showing where the ball will land. **[FACT, 2026]** the new game
  shipped an explicit **fielding reticle / shadow-visibility pass** specifically to make pop flies
  trackable, and reviewers still single out fielding as the weakest verb. The lesson is that this
  marker is load-bearing enough to be re-engineered twenty-nine years later.
  **[TARGET]** the landing marker must be readable against *every* surface we ship — pale sand,
  dark asphalt, wet cobble, shadowed alley — at 1600×900 with the ball at apex, at **≥ 3:1
  luminance contrast** against the surface directly beneath it, and it must never be smaller than
  **14 px** across.
* No ambient occlusion crevices, no shadow acne, no contact hardening. One ellipse. Soft. Done.

### 2.4 Keeping the ball readable

The ball is the most important 6 pixels in the game.

* **[TARGET]** The ball must maintain **≥ 4.5:1 luminance contrast** (WCAG relative-luminance
  formula) against whatever is behind it for the entire flight path — against sky, against foliage,
  against a brick wall, against a crowd. Measure it by taking the median of a 5×5 px patch at the
  ball's centre against the median of an 11×11 px annulus just outside its silhouette, sampled at
  **five points along the arc**: release, quarter, apex, three-quarter, landing.
* Techniques that are in-style: a thin dark outline that never disappears; a permanent bright rim;
  a short motion trail on hard-hit balls; a scale bump so the ball never renders smaller than
  **5 px** at max distance; and the ground shadow as a second, redundant read.
* **[TARGET]** In any frame where the ball is in flight, a critic should locate it in **under one
  second** without being told where to look. If they have to hunt, the frame fails Readability
  regardless of how pretty it is.

### 2.5 Light in general

* Flat, bright, midday. **No dramatic key light, no rim-lighting cinematography, no bloom.**
  **[TARGET]** the ratio between a lit plane and a shaded plane on the same object stays inside
  **1.6:1**, and ambient fill never drops below **55%** of key. One sun, high, slightly behind the
  camera; shadows short. If a critic can work out the time of day to the hour, the light is too
  cinematic.
* Everything is legible everywhere. The style has zero tolerance for "moody".
  **[TARGET]** no 64×64 px region of a daylight frame may have a median **L\* below 25**, and the
  ratio between the brightest and darkest 64×64 regions must stay **under 6:1**. That is the
  operational meaning of "there are no dark corners".
* Where a night venue exists, it is a *deliberate special occasion* with its own payoff —
  **[FACT, Backyard Baseball 2026]** hitting a home run on a night field fills the sky with
  fireworks, **one of them shaped like the hitter's own head** and another like one of that kid's
  favourite objects. Note what that costs and what it buys: a per-character firework silhouette is
  cheap art and it makes the reward *about the kid*. Night is a set-piece, never a default.
  **[TARGET]** at most **one** of our venues is a night venue, it is unlocked rather than offered,
  and its home-run payoff is per-character.

### 2.6 Edges, line, and rendering treatment

A critic staring at a PNG needs to know what the *edges* are supposed to look like, or "does this
match the style" collapses into taste. It does not have to.

* **Every character and every gameplay-critical prop carries a dark outline.** **[TARGET]** outline
  weight **1.5–3 px at 1600×900** for kids at mid-field depth, scaling with the subject's on-screen
  height so it never falls below 1 px or exceeds 4. The outline colour is a **darkened, saturated
  version of the fill it borders** (same hue ±10°, L\* reduced 35–50 points), never `#000`, and
  never a uniform grey applied to everything.
* **Background architecture takes a lighter outline or none.** **[TARGET]** the outline weight on
  buildings and boundary walls is **≤ 60%** of the character outline weight, so figures separate
  from the set in a blur test. Downsample the frame to 25% and back up: the kids must still read as
  separate objects.
* **No ambient occlusion, no contact hardening, no screen-space effects on edges.** Grounding comes
  from the contact ellipse in §2.3 and from overlap, not from a darkening gradient at every join.
* **Texture noise budget.** **[TARGET]** any surface texture must survive downsampling to 480px
  wide without turning into grey mush — i.e. its detail is at a scale of **≥ 6 px at 1600×900**.
  Fine noise is what makes a cartoon read as a render.
* **No post-processing that a 1997 sprite artist could not have painted by hand:** no bloom, no
  vignette, no chromatic aberration, no depth of field, no film grain, no colour LUT.

## 3. Environment

### 3.1 The sense of place

Backyard Baseball's fields are **somebody's actual property**, and the game never lets you forget
whose. **[FACT, 1997]** *Steele Stadium* is Ernie Steele's backyard; *Eckman Acres* is Kimmy
Eckman's. Both owners are on the playable roster, so the kid who owns the field can be standing on
it. Named ownership converts a level into a place.

**[TARGET]** Every venue we ship must be answerable to: *whose is this, and what do they do here
when nobody's playing ball?* If the answer is "it's a stickball level", the venue is not finished.

### 3.2 What actually fills the space

**[FACT, 1997]** documented venue furniture from the original fields:

* **Steele Stadium** — Ernie's backyard: the **house and driveway behind home plate**, **a shed in
  centre field that counts as a home run** (with its own catalogued sound cue, "Steele Stadium Shed
  Sproing"), **bushes in right field that make the ball hard to catch**, a fence that cuts in, and
  **the neighbour's swimming pool beyond the fence that a home-run ball can drop into**. It is the
  **smallest field in the game** and the easiest to homer in, especially to centre. (In *Backyard
  Baseball 2005* the bushes were replaced with a chain-link fence — a joke traded for a wall, and
  the field got duller for it.)
* **Tin Can Alley** — asphalt. **A large apartment complex in left field that knocks balls out of
  the air**, **a trailer in centre field**, **two dumpsters in right-centre**. Home runs are close
  to impossible without the *Aluminum Power* bat power-up; the centre fielder plays so shallow he
  stands near the shortstop; the hard surface bounces the ball unpredictably.
* **Cement Gardens** — cement surface, **squeezed between city storefronts**, and **no left-field
  wall at all**, so a routine left-side grounder can roll forever and become a home run. **[FACT,
  1997]** the four bases are four different pieces of junk: **a pizza box for home plate, a frisbee
  for first, a sewer cover for second, a sweater for third**, and the **open dumpster in centre**
  plays the same as the alley behind it. Four bases, four objects, zero of them a base — copy this
  exactly, in our own props.
* **Sandy Flats** — deep sand that **slows runners, slows fielders, and kills the ball dead**.
* **Dirt Yards** — dirt, very low left-field wall.
* **Playground Commons** — a **tall chain-link fence in right**, a **short wooden fence in left**
  separating the diamond from the actual playground equipment.
* **Eckman Acres** — Kimmy's yard: huge dimensions, a pitcher's park.
* **[FACT, 1997]** League venues escalate and the escalation is *tournament structure*, not
  decoration: **Parks Department Field** for the early rounds, **Big City Stadium** — a
  minor-league park with a big screen in centre — for the *Super Entire Nation Tournament*
  semifinals, and the **Super Colossal Dome** — AstroTurf, an unmistakable 1970s multipurpose-dome
  parody of the Kingdome / Astrodome / Metrodome — for the *Ultra-grand Championship of the
  Universe*. You do not pick these venues; you *earn* them.

Three lessons, all checkable:

1. **The junk is the geometry.** Sheds, dumpsters, trailers, pools and storefronts are not
   dressing — they are the outfield wall, the ground rules, and the jokes.
   **[TARGET]** every venue must contain **at least three named props that change play**, and each
   must be identifiable in a screenshot without a caption.
2. **Surface is a character.** Sand, cement, asphalt, dirt, lawn, turf. Each changes ball
   behaviour *and* palette *and* ambience.
3. **The venues form a ladder** from "someone's yard" to "the Dome". Scale is a reward.

### 3.3 The layer of life around the field

**[TARGET]** Every gameplay frame contains **at least 5 things doing something the player did not
cause**. In a *still*, "doing something" means caught mid-action and asymmetric — mid-stride,
mid-gesture, mid-flap, mid-pour, mid-fall — never a symmetric rest pose. In a *burst*, confirm it
by comparing two frames **0.06 s apart** (`node tools/film.mjs <scenario> --frames 2 --step 0.06`)
and checking that each of the five has moved. Candidates, in the BYB idiom, translated to ours:

* laundry moving on a line; a shutter banging; a curtain lifting in a window
* a pigeon flock crossing the upper third; a stray dog trotting the foul line
* smoke from a chimney or a chestnut cart; steam from a manhole
* a kid on a stoop who is *not* in the game, doing something else entirely
* a passing truck / a horse cart / an elevated train in the far background
* an adult leaning out a third-floor window, elbows on the sill
* a ball rolling away, a cat, a hopscotch grid, a bicycle on its side

Static props do not count toward the five. **Motion is the test.**

### 3.4 Spectators

* Spectators are **individuals, not a texture**. At the small venues there are only a handful of
  them and each one reads as a specific person with a specific posture.
* **[TARGET]** at yard-scale venues: **6–15 spectators**, each individually posed, at least
  **3 distinct silhouettes**, at least **2 of them animating** at any moment (leaning, standing up,
  waving, eating).
* **[TARGET]** Crowd behaviour is *reactive and comic*, not decorative: the crowd is a joke
  delivery system with a punchline the player triggers. At least one crowd reaction must be a
  **synchronised group gag** (everyone leans the same way, everyone ducks, everyone stands up at
  once) rather than nine independent idle loops.
* **[TARGET]** Every crowd must have a **reaction state machine** with at least four states:
  idle, lean-in (2-strike count / runner in scoring position), celebration, groan. A crowd that
  never changes state fails Aliveness.

### 3.5 Fences, boundaries, and the edge of the world

* Boundaries are **irregular and asymmetric**. Different heights, different materials, different
  distances, in the same venue. **[TARGET]** no venue may have a symmetric outfield boundary.
* The boundary is **permeable and consequential** — the ball goes over it, into a pool, off a shed,
  under a dumpster, through a window. The world does not end at the fence; it continues and the
  ball goes *into* the continuation.
* **[TARGET]** at least one boundary object per venue must produce a **unique sound and a unique
  outcome** on contact.

### 3.6 The camera, the framing, and on-screen scale

Everything in §1 and §2 is measured in *pixels of a rendered frame*, so the frame itself needs a
spec. Without one, "the head is 31% of standing height" cannot be checked, because nobody knows how
big a kid is supposed to be on screen.

* **Canonical capture.** All numbers in this file assume **1600×900, deviceScaleFactor 1** — the
  default of `node tools/shoot.mjs`. Any measurement reported at another size must say so.
* **[TARGET] Kid height on screen.** The batter and the pitcher occupy **18–26% of frame height**
  (162–234 px at 900). The deepest fielder never drops below **8% of frame height** (72 px) — below
  that, faces stop working and §1.3 becomes unscoreable. If our deep fielders are 30 px tall, the
  camera is wrong, not the characters.
* **[TARGET] Horizon and sky.** The horizon line sits in the **upper 30–45%** of the frame; the sky
  and above-horizon material occupy **25–40% of frame area**. Under 20% sky and the frame turns
  into a floor plan; over 50% and it turns into a landscape painting with kids in it.
* **[TARGET] Camera pitch.** A three-quarter view, **20–35° above the horizontal**. Flat-on is a
  side-scroller; over 45° is a tactics game. Field of view stays inside **35–55°** so the street
  does not fish-eye.
* **[TARGET] Depth layers.** Every gameplay frame resolves into at least **four** readable depth
  bands — foreground occluder (a stoop rail, a parked cart, a lamppost base), play plane, mid
  ground (facades, spectators), far ground (rooftops, the elevated, sky). At least **one**
  foreground element must overlap the play plane without hiding the ball or the batter.
* **[FACT, 1997]** The game changes camera by *situation*, not by cinematography: a batting view for
  the at-bat, and a wide view of the whole field once the ball is hit, so every runner and every
  fielder is visible at once. Our camera changes for the same reason — to keep information on
  screen — and for no other.
* **[TARGET] Nothing gameplay-critical inside 4% of any frame edge.** Ball, count, batter and the
  landing marker all stay inside that margin.

## 4. UI and typography

### 4.1 The overall character of the interface

Every HUD element in a Backyard interface is a **depicted physical object with a material**, not a
graphic primitive. That is the whole of "scrappy and hand-made", and it is checkable one element at
a time: point at a HUD element and name the material it is made of. Chalk. Painted tin. Slate.
Card. Rope. Enamel. If the honest answer is "a rectangle", that element fails.

**[TARGET]** Every persistent HUD element must satisfy all five:
1. **Names a material** (see above), and shows at least one artefact of it — a chipped edge, a
   chalk smudge, a nail head, a torn corner, a rust patch, a lettering wobble.
2. **Has a non-rectangular or non-uniform outline** — no element may be an axis-aligned rectangle
   with all four corners equal.
3. **Sits at a rotation of ±1–4°** from frame axes, or is visibly hand-placed rather than gridded.
4. **Uses a hue drawn from the frame's own palette** (§2.1), not a UI grey. **Zero** HUD pixels may
   be a neutral grey with S < 0.10.
5. **Uses no default system font** — see §4.4.

Banned outright: pure-grey rounded rectangles, hairline strokes under 1.5 px, drop shadows at 0.1
opacity, progress bars, and any icon that looks like it came from a UI kit. If a frame's HUD would
look at home in a productivity app, it fails Cohesion.

### 4.2 The scorebug and the count

* **[FACT, 1997]** The pitcher's stamina meter is drawn as a **juice box** and the resource is
  *called* "juice". It drains gradually with every pitch and **faster with each special pitch**;
  when it runs low the pitcher's **accuracy starts to waver**, which is your cue to go to the pen;
  and the **More Juice** power-up refills it completely. This is the single most instructive UI
  decision in the game: the most abstract quantity in the sim is rendered as **the most concrete
  object a nine-year-old owns**, and the *consequence* of it emptying is visible in the pitching,
  not in a number.
  **[TARGET]** every numeric quantity in our HUD is expressed as a *thing*, not a bar: a chalk
  tally, a soda bottle, a stack of bottle caps, clothespins on a line, notches cut into a stoop.
  And every such thing must have a **visible behavioural consequence** when it empties.
* **[FACT, Backyard Baseball 2001]** Player condition is colour-coded on the in-game scoreboard:
  **orange = good day, green = average day, blue = bad day**, with **plus/minus baseballs** showing
  the stat adjustment. A kid's day is swung by *who their teammates are, which field they are on,
  their uniform number and colour, and whether it is their birthday.* Colour carries state, exactly
  as our own design system requires — and the causes are all things a child finds meaningful.
  Note the attribution: the original 1997 game has no such indicator.
* **[TARGET]** The count (balls / strikes / outs) must be readable at **a 3-second glance from
  across a room**, i.e. legible when the screenshot is downsampled to **480px wide**. Test it by
  downsampling.
* Base occupancy is a **diamond diagram** with filled/empty corners — the single most
  universally-understood piece of baseball UI ever drawn. Ours must be equally instant, and it
  should be made of period material (chalk on brick, a scratched slate, a hand-lettered card).
* The scorebug is **small and cornered**, never a banner across the top. The field is the star.
  **[TARGET]** total persistent HUD area **≤ 12% of the frame**, split across **no more than three
  clusters**, each anchored to a corner and each inside a **4% safe margin**. No HUD element may
  overlap the batter, the pitcher, the ball, or the landing marker at any point in a normal at-bat.
* **[FACT, 1997]** The most important piece of gameplay UI is not the scorebug — it is the **grey
  circle showing where the pitch will cross the plate**, visible to *both* the batter and the
  pitcher. It is the whole mind-game rendered as one shape, and it is shared information.
  **[TARGET]** our equivalent is a single chalk-drawn ring, **≥ 40 px across at 1600×900**, at
  **≥ 3:1** contrast against the wall behind it, present in every pitch frame, and legible to both
  sides. A critic who cannot find it in a pitch screenshot has found a fail.

### 4.3 Menus, team select, player cards

* **[FACT, 1997]** The main menu is not a menu — it is **the Clubhouse**, a rendered room you click
  around in. The **bulletin board** starts League Play; the game's three modes are **Pick-Up Game,
  League Play and Batting Practice**; and the **trophy case** holds the records, the trophies and
  the players' baseball cards. **Shift-clicking Mr. Clanky inside the trophy case** unlocks the
  robot as a playable character, and doing it correctly plays a confirmation sound. Menus are
  *places with secrets in them*, and the secret has audio feedback.
  **[TARGET]** our front end must be a place, and it must contain **at least one hidden
  interaction** that rewards clicking something that isn't a button — and that interaction must
  confirm itself with a sound, because an unconfirmed secret reads as a bug.
* **[OBSERVED]** Team selection is a **draft of 9 kids from the pool of 30**, presented as picking
  kids who are waiting to be picked. The playground ritual of being chosen is the interface.
* **[FACT, 1997]** Player cards carry a portrait, a **nickname**, a **birthday**, batting hand,
  throwing hand, and four ratings — **batting / running / pitching / fielding — on a 1–4 scale,
  drawn as baseballs**. Four axes, four pips. Pablo is 4 in batting, fielding and running and
  "only" a 3 in pitching; Kenny carries a 2 in batting and is the best arm in the game.
  **[FACT, 2001]** the same four axes were later re-scaled to 1–10 — the axes never changed, only
  the resolution, which is the right thing to expand and the wrong thing to start with.
  **[TARGET]** our stat display is **exactly 4 axes** on a **4-point scale**, drawn as discrete
  physical pips (chalk marks, bottle caps, stars), never a continuous bar and never a two-digit
  number. Four pips is legible at 96 px; ten is not.
* **[FACT, 1997]** Team names are built by combining an adjective with a noun. The ten stock team
  names are the **All-Stars, Bombers, Fishes, Giants, Hornets, Melonheads, Monsters, Rockets,
  Socks and Wombats** — and picking "Red" or "White" with "Socks" is a deliberate joke waiting for
  the player to find it. The game hands the player a joke generator and lets them author the joke.
* **[FACT, 1997]** Nicknames are canon and are used in the UI: Pablo **"The Secret Weapon"**,
  Kenny **"K-Man"**, Billy Jean **"Crazy Legs"**, Gretchen **"Jabberjaw"**, Dmitri **"Paste"**,
  Ernie **"Funnybones"/"Big Easy"**, Vicki **"Swan"**, Achmed **"Axeman"**, and the Webber twins
  who *share* **"Little Smokey"**.
  **[TARGET]** every one of our kids ships with a nickname, it appears in the frame at least once
  per at-bat, and at least one nickname is shared by two kids — shared names are free jokes.

### 4.4 Typography

* **Hand-drawn or hand-lettered display type** with visible irregularity — uneven baselines,
  varying stroke weight, letters that lean. Chunky. High x-height. Generous letter spacing.
* **[TARGET]** No glyph in a title or a scorebug may be perfectly vertical and perfectly uniform.
  Introduce **±1.5° rotation jitter**, **±3% scale jitter** and **±2% baseline offset** per glyph on
  display text. The jitter must be **seeded and stable**, not per-frame — jitter that re-rolls every
  frame reads as a rendering bug, not as a hand.
* Numbers are the exception: the count and the score must be **maximally legible**, so use the
  hand-lettered face at large size rather than a decorative face at small size.
* **[TARGET]** Two type sizes on screen at once, three at the absolute maximum, and **at most two
  typefaces** in the whole game. Backyard UIs are not information-dense.
* **[TARGET]** Every glyph that carries state (the count, the score, the inning) is **≥ 24 px tall
  at 1600×900** and holds **≥ 4.5:1** contrast against the material it is painted on.
* Text sits on **a physical ground** — a card, a plank, a chalked patch, a taped label — never
  floating on a translucent panel.
* **Period note for us:** 1920s NYC gives us signpainter scripts, condensed wood-type, gold-leaf
  storefront lettering, and chalk. All of these are hand-made and all are period-true. Use them.
  A geometric sans is an anachronism *and* a style violation — a two-for-one failure.

### 4.5 The sound of the interface

**[FACT, 1997]** The interface is *audibly* physical. Event sounds are lifted from the classic
cartoon libraries — Sound Ideas, Hollywood Edge — with catalogued cues including
`Sound Ideas, BOINK, CARTOON - TIMP DOINK` (a kid landing on the bat),
`Sound Ideas, HIT, CARTOON - SHRILL BILP` (in the intro),
`Sound Ideas, TAKE, CARTOON - WHISTLE TAKE`, and a **descending slide whistle**.

**[TARGET]** Every clickable element makes a *mechanical or comic* sound: a wooden clack, a boing,
a slide whistle, a pop, a rattle of caps. **Zero** synthesised UI blips. If it sounds like a
notification, it is wrong.

**[FACT, 1997]** One instructive cut: Sunny Day was recorded reading out the menu text, and the
feature was dropped — the shipped menus are **silent of speech**, and the orphaned lines still sit
at the very top of the dialogue file where they were moved and forgotten. Voice narration of UI was
built, tried, and judged unnecessary. Sound design carries the menus; talking does not.

## 5. Animation vocabulary

### 5.1 Frame budget and general principles

Assume a **12 fps animation feel on a 60 fps render** — poses held, transitions quick, no
interpolated mush. Every action has a **pose you could freeze and screenshot**.

**[TARGET]** For any action, a frame burst must contain at least **three distinct, readable,
screenshot-worthy poses**. Capture it with the tool that exists rather than by hand:
`node tools/film.mjs <scenario> --frames 12 --step 0.033` gives a numbered contact sheet at 1/30 s
steps. If consecutive frames look like linear interpolation between two states, the animation is
dead, and a contact sheet makes that obvious in one read where a video does not.

Beat budget (at 60 fps), the numbers a critic should measure in a burst:

| Beat | Frames @60 | What must be visible |
|---|---|---|
| Anticipation before any major action | **8–14** | weight shift *away* from the action |
| Windup (pitcher) | **24–40** | leg lift, arm coil, a held apex pose |
| Pitch release → plate | **18–30** | ball scale/trail change, arm follow-through |
| Swing (start → contact) | **6–10** | bat blur arc, hips leading hands |
| Contact hitstop | **3–6** | *everything stops*, ball deforms, impact burst |
| Swing follow-through | **12–20** | bat wraps, kid's head turns to track the ball |
| Run cycle | **10–16 per stride** | 2-frame airborne pose, arms crossing the body |
| Slide | **20–30** | dust plume, a held skid pose, a beat of stillness at the end |
| Dive/catch | **16–26** | full extension held for ≥4 frames, then landing squash |
| Celebration | **45–90** | at least two distinct gestures, not one loop |
| Dejection | **30–60** | slump held, slow recovery |

### 5.2 Squash, stretch, anticipation, follow-through

* **The ball squashes on contact.** **[TARGET]** deform to **1.35–1.6× along the velocity axis**
  for **2–4 frames** at contact and at any hard bounce. A rigid sphere is a hard fail on Feel.
* **Kids squash on landing and stretch on leaps.** A kid coming down from a jump compresses to
  **~0.85 height / 1.15 width** for 3 frames before recovering.
* **Anticipation is non-negotiable.** Nothing moves forward without first moving back. Every
  swing has a load. Every throw has a coil. Every sprint has a lean-back-then-launch.
* **Follow-through and overlap.** Hair, cap brims, shirt hems, ponytails, tutu skirts, glove
  laces and untied laces all **lag the body by 2–4 frames**. **[TARGET]** every kid carries at
  least **one secondary element** that overlaps. This is what makes the difference between a
  puppet and a character, and it is visible in a two-frame comparison.

### 5.3 Idle fidgets — the most under-built and most valuable animation in the game

Nine kids stand still for most of a baseball game. What they do while standing still *is* the game's
personality.

**[TARGET]** Every kid has **at least three idle fidgets** on a randomised timer, and the fidget
set is **character-specific**, not shared. In BYB terms: Pete's tongue lolls out; Kenny pops a
wheelie; Vicki does a small ballet position; Tony fixes his hair; Dmitri pushes his glasses up;
Marky scratches a bare foot with the other foot; Kimmy blows a bubble.

**[TARGET]** Across a 10-second static capture of a fielding scene, **no two kids may be in the
same pose at the same time**, and at least **three** must visibly move.

### 5.4 The at-bat, beat by beat

1. **Walk-up.** **[FACT, 1997]** Batters have **per-character walk-up animations** — Achmed
   air-guitars his way to the plate with the bat. **[OBSERVED]** and here is the criticism, which
   matters more than the praise: reviewers of the 2024 re-release note that *the same walk-up plays
   every single time*, and that the game is slow partly because of it.
   **[TARGET]** every kid gets a walk-up, **each kid gets at least two variants**, the animation is
   **≤ 2.0 s**, and it is **interruptible by input from frame 1**. A unique walk-up you cannot skip
   becomes a tax on the twentieth at-bat.
2. **Stance settle.** A held, characterful, technically-wrong pose. Readable at 96px.
3. **Pitcher windup.** Big, slow, telegraphed. The apex is a *held pose*.
4. **Flight.** Ball trail plus landing marker. The special pitches are **visual events** —
   **[FACT, 1997]** the **Fireball** is literally a ball of fire travelling at high speed; the
   **Corkscrew** loops the ball in circles on its way in; **Zig-Zag** alternates left and right;
   **Big Freeze** blazes in and then stops dead at the plate; the **Elevator** starts below the
   strike zone and rises as you swing; **Crazyball** goes anywhere but straight; the **Laughing
   Ball** audibly laughs; the **Spitball** is slathered in spit and drifts off line; **Slo-Mo** is
   a very slow ball. Note that seven of the nine are legible from *shape of path* alone.
   **[TARGET]** every special pitch of ours must be identifiable from a **single still frame** of
   its flight, without the trail — the ball's own treatment must carry it.
5. **Swing.** 6–10 frames, motion-blurred arc, hips before hands.
6. **Contact.** See 5.5.
7. **Reaction.** The batter's head snaps to track the ball; fielders react *before* the camera does.

### 5.5 What the camera does on contact and on a home run

* **Hitstop.** **[TARGET]** on solid contact, freeze the simulation for **60–100 ms** (4–6 frames).
  This single effect is worth more Feel points than any amount of particle work.
* **Impact burst** at the bat: a radial white/yellow star or dust puff, **4–6 frames**, plus a
  **screen shake of 3–6 px amplitude decaying over 8–12 frames**. Scale all three with exit velocity.
* **Camera on contact:** a fast **snap-out** (widen 8–15% over 6 frames) then a smooth track of
  the ball. The cut from the batter's view to the field view should be **immediate**, not a lerp.
* **[OBSERVED]** On a hit, the game pulls out to a view of the **whole field** so you can see every
  runner and every fielder at once. Readability beats cinematography. **[TARGET]** within 10 frames
  of contact, all runners and the ball must be simultaneously visible, and no runner may be closer
  than 24 px to a frame edge.
* **Home run:** the camera **follows the ball out of the world** — over the fence, off the shed,
  into the pool, through the window — and *the world reacts*. Then a **celebration cut**:
  **[FACT, 1997]** the kid's own theme music plays as they round the bases. **[FACT, Backyard
  Baseball 2026]** every kid has a distinct home-run celebration — Stephanie Morgan dances the
  Griddy — and on a night field the sky fills with fireworks shaped like the hitter's head.
  **[TARGET]** a home run occupies **2.5–5 seconds** of spectacle before play resumes, it is
  **per-character in at least two channels** (animation + music sting), and it is skippable after
  **1.2 s** — long enough to feel like a reward the first time, short enough to survive the tenth.
* **[FACT, 1997]** Slow motion is scored for comedy, not for awe: the cue is a stock library
  **woman's scream** (`Hollywoodedge, Woman Screamsahhh M CRT028203`, from Cartoon Trax Vol. 1),
  the same asset used for the **Screaming Line Drive** power-up. Slow-mo in this style is a *joke
  device*. If our slow motion sounds expensive, it is wrong.

### 5.6 Fielding, running, and comic failure

* **Run cycles are bouncy and inefficient.** Big vertical travel, arms crossing the midline,
  knees high. **[TARGET]** vertical bob amplitude ≈ **6–10% of kid height** per stride. Kids do
  not glide.
* **Slides raise dust and end in a held pose.** The dust plume outlives the slide by ~10 frames.
* **Dives hold full extension.** **[TARGET]** the fully-extended apex is held for **≥ 4 frames**
  with zero root motion, and the pose is composed to be croppable as a poster: arm, ball, hat and
  ground shadow all inside frame, limbs not overlapping the torso.
* **Failure is animated as carefully as success.** **[OBSERVED]** fielders drop routine flies,
  fall over, and get confused by balls landing between the infield and the outfield — errors are
  frequent enough that **the game ships an option to turn errors off entirely**, which tells you
  the designers knew they were a *feature with a volume knob*, not a bug.
  **[FACT, 1997]** when fielders collide, Vinnie calls **"Pig pile! Everybody jump on!"** — the
  collision is a designed, animated, *voiced* event.
  **[TARGET]** we ship at least **four** distinct comic-failure animations (bobble, collision,
  through-the-legs, ball-lands-between-two-kids-who-both-stopped), each with its own audio call,
  and — copying the 1997 options menu — **a settings toggle that turns comic errors off**.
* **Celebrations are group events.** Teammates converge. **[TARGET]** a scoring play pulls at
  least **three** kids into a shared celebration, not one kid emoting alone.

## 6. Audio

### 6.1 The announce booth

**[FACT, 1997]** Two commentators, and the pairing is the whole architecture:

* **Sunny Day** — play-by-play. A girl. Bright, fast, technically correct, relentlessly
  enthusiastic. Voiced by **Jen Taylor** (later Cortana in *Halo*), who also voiced several of the
  kids in the same game — Billy Jean Blackwood and Luanne Lui among them. Her home-run call is
  **"Goodbye, baseball!"**
* **Vinnie the Gooch** — colour. Modelled on the sportscaster **Howard Cosell**: a
  Brooklyn-accented, deadpan, third-person-referring wiseguy — and **voiced by a woman**,
  **Dolores Rogers**. Mellow where Sunny is hot. He is the one who is funny.

**[FACT] Vinnie lines verified against transcripts**, which are the tonal specification:

> "Vinny da Gooch is lovin' that!"
> "It was in there like swimwear." *(on a pitch)*
> "Pig pile! Everybody jump on!" *(fielder collision)*
> "I wouldn't want to meet this kid in a dark alley. I wouldn't want to meet him in a light
> alley, either. Alleys are scary!" *(a generic kid steps up)*

And the two canonical two-handers:

> **Sunny:** "Hi ho!" — **Vinnie:** "Yo." — **Sunny:** "Whatever, Vinnie."

> **Sunny:** "Say 'hello', Vinnie."
> **Vinnie:** "Hello, Vinnie."

**[OBSERVED]** Widely quoted in fan transcripts but not verified against a primary source — use
them as tone, never cite them as fact: *"Aloha means goodbye, baby."*, *"I don't know what kind of
pitch that was, but it tasted just like chicken!"*, *"Sweet as candy I tell ya! I like candy,
everybody likes candy."*

**Why these work, stated as rules a writer can follow:**
1. **The joke lands one beat after the information.** Call the play straight, *then* be strange.
2. **The non sequitur is about food, weather, or personal cowardice.** Never about baseball.
3. **Vinnie undercuts his own bit immediately** ("Alleys are scary!"). He is not above the joke.
4. **He refers to himself in the third person** and it is never explained.
5. **Sunny never acknowledges the joke.** The straight man does not blink. That is why it's funny.
6. **The banter is treated as load-bearing inside the fiction.** The announcers are written as
   neighbourhood celebrities the kids talk about, not as a layer over the top of the game.
7. **[FACT, Backyard Football]** The straight-man gag is franchise-wide and it survives a change of
   partner: Chuck Downfield is so forgetful he keeps calling Sunny **"Sammy"**, and when she
   corrects him with her actual name he answers **"It sure is. I should have brought my
   sunglasses!"** — a homonym misfire held for a full extra beat, from a man who has already
   forgotten her name once.

**[TARGET] Line inventory for our announcer**, minimum, or the booth will repeat inside one game:
* **12** strike calls, **10** ball calls, **12** out calls
* **10** base-hit calls, **6** double, **4** triple, **8** home run
* **6** great-defensive-play, **6** comic-error, **4** collision
* **8** between-innings, **6** score-is-lopsided, **6** score-is-close-late
* **1 unique reference line per kid** — the announcers must know their names *and* their deals.
* **[FACT, 1997]** For the 100 unnamed generic kids the only thing Sunny says is the generic
  **"steps up to bat"** — and the per-player variants she recorded went *unused*, sitting in the
  files until the later MLB-licensed editions used them. Ship the fallback, but never let it be the
  only thing a critic hears, and never record variants you have no trigger for.

### 6.2 Kid chatter

* **[OBSERVED]** Reviewers single this out: "the kids are always chanting"; "the sound, from the
  children's taunts to the birds chirping, is excellent."
  **[FACT, series]** Chatter is treated as a **separate, addressable audio layer** in the options
  menu: *Backyard Baseball '01* exposes independent on/off toggles for background music, background
  sound and commentary, and *Backyard Sports Baseball 2007* ships an explicit **"Chatter" volume
  control**. The developers considered kid noise its own bus. So do we.
* Chatter is **infield noise**: encouragement to your own pitcher, needling of the batter,
  celebration, groans. It runs **underneath** the announcers, not in the gaps between them.
* **[TARGET]** chatter events fire **every 2–5 seconds** during live play, mixed **8–12 dB** under
  the announcer, ducking to **-18 dB** while an announcer line plays.
* **[FACT, 1997–2003]** Characters keep their languages, untranslated and unsubtitled: Pablo speaks
  Spanish in every game from 1997 onward, and it is not until **Backyard Baseball 2003** that he
  reveals he had been fluent in English the whole time and learned the *Spanish* at school. Six
  years of a straight-faced bit before the payoff — that is the patience the joke was given.
  **[TARGET]** at least two of our kids speak something other than English on the field,
  untranslated, and it is never a punchline about them.
* Each kid has a **pick-me line**, an **intro line**, **player-talk** and **chatter** sets. The
  wiki structure of the original game's dialogue is literally organised this way.

### 6.3 The crack of the bat

* **[TARGET]** three tiers, and they must be distinguishable blind: **weak contact** (dull thock,
  short), **solid contact** (sharp crack, ~120 ms, bright transient), **home run** (crack + a
  low-end thump + a brief tail). Contact quality must be **audible before it is visible**.
* Our period gives us a **broomstick on a rubber ball** — a hollow, woody *tock*, not a
  aluminium ping and not an ash-bat crack. Get this one sound right and half the period work
  is done.
* **[FACT, 1997]** Boundary objects have their own named cues — **"Steele Stadium Shed Sproing"**
  is a catalogued sound asset, i.e. one shed on one field earned its own file with its own name.
  **[TARGET]** every boundary prop gets a bespoke impact sound: iron rail, fire escape, storefront
  glass, ash can, cobblestone, wooden crate — and each one is named in the asset list after the
  object *and the venue*, so nobody can quietly reuse it.

### 6.4 Ambience

**[FACT, 1997]** The game shipped **per-field ambient beds** — archivists have catalogued distinct
ambience for *Cement Gardens*; *Steele Stadium / Playground Commons / Eckman Acres* (one shared
bed across three lawn venues); *Dirt Yards*; *Sandy Flats*; *Tin Can Alley*; and *Parks Department
Field*. Note the economy: **six beds for nine-plus venues**, shared where the surface and setting
match. Ambience is **venue identity**, not global filler, and identity can be shared where the
place genuinely is the same kind of place.

**[TARGET]** each venue gets a bed of **4–6 looping layers at different periods** so the loop never
becomes audible: for us — distant elevated train, a horse cart, a hurdy-gurdy or a radio through a
window, pigeons, a foghorn from the river, kids on another block, a shopkeeper shouting.
**[TARGET]** at least one ambience layer is **sporadic** (fires every 20–60 s), because that is
what stops a loop from sounding like a loop.

### 6.5 Sound effects palette

**[FACT, 1997]** The game's SFX are **stock cartoon library** cues — Sound Ideas and Hollywood
Edge. Documented examples, with the moments they are attached to:
`Hollywoodedge, Woman Screamsahhh M CRT028203` (slow motion, and the Screaming Line Drive);
`Sound Ideas, BOINK, CARTOON - TIMP DOINK` (Dante Robinson landing on the bat);
`Sound Ideas, HIT, CARTOON - SHRILL BILP` (the intro);
`Sound Ideas, TAKE, CARTOON - WHISTLE TAKE` (Dante flipping a hot dog).
The pattern worth copying is not the library — it is that **every one of those cues is welded to a
specific, nameable, animated moment**, not to a generic event class.

**[TARGET]** Our SFX vocabulary is **comic-physical**: boings, doinks, slide whistles, timpani
hits, ratchets, whip cracks, rubber squeaks, cartoon takes. **No** synthesised whooshes, **no**
cinematic braams, **no** sub-bass impacts. This is a cartoon and it must sound like one.

### 6.6 Music

* **[FACT, 1997]** Composer **Rhett Mathis** wrote **an individual theme song for all 30
  characters**, each derived from that kid's personality, and they play **when you click a kid's
  card and when that kid rounds the bases after a home run**. Documented examples: Pablo's theme is
  built on **"España cañí"**, Pascual Marquina Narro's 1920s pasodoble; Pete "had a drawl that was
  sort of cowboyish", so his is a "wandering, weird, piano honky-tonky kind of piece, but slow, like
  an old cowboy"; **Achmed's is an homage to Van Halen's "Jump"**; Lisa Crockett's is a disjointed
  trombone number; Kiesha's evokes a late-night TV theme.
  **[FACT, 1997]** Mikey Thomas's middle name is "Rhett", after Rhett Mathis — who was also the
  original game's programmer. The composer is in the roster.
* **This is the highest-leverage audio idea in the entire reference.** A per-character musical
  sting is how a game makes you *love* a roster. Note also *where* it fires: on the card and on the
  home-run trot — the two moments the game most wants you to attach to a person.
  **[TARGET]** every kid gets a **3–6 second sting** in a distinct instrumentation and mode, fired
  in **three** places: the card, the walk-up, and the home-run trot. For 1920s NYC: a klezmer
  clarinet, a tinny ragtime piano, a Neapolitan mandolin, a lone cornet, a jaw harp, a hurdy-gurdy,
  a bass drum-and-cymbal one-man band, a stride-piano lick. Each kid's sting must be nameable by
  instrument alone in a blind listen.
* **When music plays vs. drops out:** full themes on the front end, the draft, and results screens.
  During live play, music **gets out of the way** — either silent under ambience and chatter, or a
  thin bed at **-20 dB**. Music returns for the walk-up sting, the home run, and the end of the
  inning. **[TARGET]** the loudest thing during a pitch is the crowd and the kids, never the score.

## 7. Game feel and pacing

### 7.1 The clock

* **[TARGET]** A complete at-bat: **20–40 seconds**. A half inning: **90–150 seconds**.
  A full game: **12–20 minutes**. If our game exceeds these, we have a pacing bug, not a design.
* **[TARGET]** Dead time between pitches: **≤ 3 seconds** before *something* happens — a chatter
  line, an idle fidget, a crowd reaction, an announcer aside, a pigeon. The player is nine.
  Three seconds of silence is an eternity.
* **[OBSERVED]** The 1997 game is not fast, and reviewers of the 2024 re-release say so plainly:
  "fun, goofy, but a slow-paced experience"; there *is* a skip button for animations and walk-ins
  and "it doesn't feel like enough"; the announcers repeat lines far more than a modern game would;
  the same walk-up animation plays every time. **Learn from the criticism, not just the praise** —
  long animations are charming for about ten repetitions and a tax thereafter.
  **[TARGET]** every celebratory or announcer beat is **interruptible by input from frame 1**, no
  non-interruptible beat exceeds **1.2 s**, and any beat that fires more than five times per game
  has **at least three variants**.

### 7.2 What the player actually does

**[FACT, 1997]** The control surface, which is a masterclass in "few verbs, real depth":

* **Batting:** four swing types — **Power, Line Drive, Grounder, Bunt** — plus a **stance toggle**
  on the small circle below home plate (**open** hits inside pitches better, **square** is best over
  the middle, **closed** is best on pitches away), plus placement via a cursor. A **grey circle**
  shows where the pitch will cross the plate, **visible to both batter and pitcher** — shared
  information, which is what makes it a mind-game rather than a reflex test.
* **Pitching:** **heat, slowballs and hooks**, plus placement, plus whatever special pitch you have
  earned — reviewers count roughly **a dozen** pitch options in total once specials are unlocked.
* **Fielding:** click where you want a fielder to go; the nearest kid runs there to meet the ball.
* Everything is **mouse-first, one-click**, with no tutorial required.

**[TARGET]** Our verb count matches: **≤ 5 offensive options, ≤ 5 defensive options**, each
distinguishable by *outcome shape* rather than by numbers. A child must be able to explain the
whole control scheme to another child in under a minute.

### 7.3 The power-up layer

**[FACT, 1997]** This is the arcade engine underneath the sandlot, and its economy is elegant:

* Strike a batter out on **three straight pitches** and you earn a **special pitch**. Throwing one
  **costs juice** (stamina). Reward and cost are in the same currency loop.
* **[FACT, 1997]** Nine special pitches, exactly: **Fireball** (a literal ball of fire at high
  speed), **Slo-Mo** (a very slow ball), **Corkscrew** (circles the ball inward to bait a swing),
  **Zig-Zag** (alternates left and right), **Crazyball** (goes anywhere but straight), **Laughing
  Ball** (audibly laughs), **Big Freeze** (blazes in, then stops dead at the plate), **Elevator**
  (starts low, rises over the bat mid-swing), **Spitball** (covered in spit, drifts off line).
* **Hit a special pitch and the batter earns a power-up**, matched to the swing type used:
  **Aluminum Power** (power swing → guaranteed home run), **Screaming Line Drive** (line drive →
  screaming liner off the wall), **Under Grounder** (grounder), **Crazy Bunt** (bunt → ball behaves
  chaotically and fielders can't handle it). **More Juice** fully refills a pitcher's stamina.
* **[FACT, 1997]** **Mr. Clanky** the robot is already in the 1997 game as the **Batting Practice
  pitcher**, and he becomes playable when you **shift-click him in the Clubhouse trophy case**. His
  card description is written in **hexadecimal**, and it decodes to *"Mr. Clanky says, 'Stay in
  school.'"* — a secret inside a secret, aimed squarely at the one kid who will bother.
  **[FACT, 2001/2003]** in the later entries he has **infinite juice**: he is the joke ceiling of
  the whole stamina system.

**Design lessons, all checkable:**
1. **The counter-play is symmetric.** The pitcher's reward creates the batter's opportunity.
   Nobody is ever purely on the receiving end of a power-up.
2. **Every power-up is a visual and audio spectacle first, a stat second.**
   **[TARGET]** each of ours is identifiable from one still frame and one 500 ms audio clip.
3. **Names are jokes.** "Under Grounder", "Crazy Bunt", "Big Freeze". Not "Power Shot Lv.2".
4. **[TARGET]** we ship **6–9 special pitches** and **4–5 batting power-ups**, earned by an
   in-game achievement, spent from a visible physical resource.

### 7.4 Difficulty, mercy, and the ethic

* **[FACT, franchise]** Humongous was an *edutainment* studio and Backyard Sports was that applied
  to sport: the games exist to **teach the rules** and to **welcome a kid regardless of skill**,
  with equal representation of girls and boys who are all genuinely good at something. The point
  was never to sell a kid an unattainable big-league dream. Not about stats. Not about being best.
* **[FACT, 1997]** Everyone is good at something and the 1–4 stat spread proves it: Pablo is
  **4 batting / 4 fielding / 4 running and "only" 3 pitching**; Kiesha Phillips has elite power
  *and* speed; Kenny Kawaguchi is the best arm in the game with a **2** in batting; Angela
  Delvecchio has strong pitching, subpar running and poor fielding; Marky Dubois is hardy but slow;
  Reese Worthington is small, frail and asthmatic.
  **[TARGET]** **no kid on our roster is bad at everything**, and **no kid is good at everything**
  except one deliberate legend. Every kid must have at least one **4** and at least one **1 or 2**,
  and the legend caps at 4/4/4/3 exactly as Pablo does — even the best kid is missing something.
* **[OBSERVED]** The 1997 game is easy and reviewers said so. That is a *feature* at the low end
  and a *flaw* at the high end. **[TARGET]** the floor must be gentle enough that a child never
  gets shut out, and the ceiling must be interesting enough that an adult replays the season.
* **[OBSERVED]** Fielding outcomes are substantially out of the player's hands after a hit — errors
  are common, fielders fall over, and the same play resolves differently on different attempts.
  **[FACT, 1997]** the options menu lets you **turn errors off**, which is the tell: the randomness
  is a deliberate, *removable* mercy that protects a child from believing a loss was their fault.
  Tune it as mercy, ship the switch, and never let the randomness decide a game the player was
  winning by more than three.
* Weakness is written as **personality, never as pity**. The asthmatic kid, the wheelchair user,
  the kid with two left feet — all of them are *good at the game* in some dimension, and the text
  never apologises for them.

### 7.5 How it keeps a nine-year-old engaged

Rank-ordered, because the order matters for where our effort goes:

1. **Someone is always talking.** Announcer, chatter, crowd. **[TARGET]** no gap over **3 s**
   without a voice event during live play.
2. **Something is always moving** that the player didn't cause. **[TARGET]** ≥ 5 per frame (§3.3).
3. **A named favourite is on screen** and the game keeps saying their name. **[TARGET]** a kid's
   name or nickname is on screen or in audio **at least once per at-bat**.
4. **The next spectacle is visibly close** — the juice box, the strikeout counter, the power-up.
   **[TARGET]** at least one HUD object shows *progress toward a reward* at all times.
5. **The joke is renewable.** **[TARGET]** the line inventory in §6.1 is the floor; no line repeats
   inside a single game.
6. **The player authored something** — the team name, the roster, the custom kid. **[TARGET]** at
   least **two** authored choices are visible in a gameplay frame (team name on a card, chosen
   roster on the field).

## 8. Humour

Backyard Baseball is **funny**, not merely cute. The distinction is the whole thing, and it is
achievable in code.

### 8.1 Why it is funny rather than cute

* **Cute** is a property of a design. **Funny** is a property of an *event*. Cute is a still frame;
  funny requires a before and an after. Every joke below has a setup and a beat.
  **[TARGET]** the test is mechanical: for any gag we ship, write down the **setup frame** and the
  **payoff frame** and the number of seconds between them. If you cannot name two frames, you have
  built a decoration, not a joke — and decorations do not get funnier on the tenth viewing.
* **The game commits to bits with a straight face.** The Laughing Ball is not winked at. Vinnie's
  third-person self-reference is never explained. Pablo's Spanish is never subtitled.
* **The comedy is generous, never mean.** The kids are laughed *with*. Nobody's disability,
  ethnicity, gender, or weight is ever the joke. The jokes are about food, cowardice, and physics.

### 8.2 The specific joke inventory

**Verbal / announcer**
* Deadpan non sequitur one beat after the call ("...tasted just like chicken!").
* Self-undercutting escalation ("I wouldn't want to meet him in a light alley either.
  Alleys are scary!").
* Literal-minded misreading of an instruction ("Say hello, Vinnie." / "Hello, Vinnie.").
* Homonym confusion held for a full extra beat ("That's *Sunny*." / "It sure is.").
* Third-person self-reference, unexplained, forever.
* Food obsession as a running gag (candy, chicken).

**Physical**
* Fielders colliding into a pile — and the pile being *called* by the booth.
* A ball landing between two kids who both stopped running.
* A routine pop fly dropped by a kid standing directly under it.
* Cartoon-library `BOINK` when a kid lands on the bat.
* A slide that overshoots the base entirely.
* The physical comedy of the special pitches themselves — a ball that *laughs*, a ball that
  freezes in mid-air, a ball that rises over the bat as you swing at it.

**Environmental**
* The home run that lands in **the neighbour's swimming pool**.
* The shed in centre field that counts as a home run and goes **sproing**.
* The dumpsters, the trailer, the apartment block that knocks your home run out of the sky.
* Bases made of pizza boxes.

**Systemic / meta**
* The adjective+noun team-name generator, whose stock nouns include **Melonheads** and **Wombats**,
  and which quietly rewards a player who types "Red" + "Socks".
* **[FACT, 1997]** **100 generic background kids — 50 pairs with identical stats** — many named
  after members of the development team. The duplication is invisible until you look for it, and
  then it is funny.
* **[FACT, 1997]** Mr. Clanky, hidden behind a shift-click in the trophy case, with a card
  description written in hexadecimal that decodes to "Mr. Clanky says, 'Stay in school.'"
* Nicknames that are gently absurd: **"Jabberjaw"**, **"Paste"**, **"Crazy Legs"**, **"Funnybones"**,
  and one nickname (**"Little Smokey"**) shared by two identical twins.

**Character-level running gags** (verified; the template is exact)
* **[FACT, Backyard Football]** Stephanie Morgan is so baseball-obsessed that she turns up at
  *football* tryouts — "Hi, I'm Stephanie Morgan! Is this the right place for baseball tryouts? Oh,
  football? I guess that's okay too" — and then spends her dialogue arguing that football is
  basically baseball anyway. The gag is that **she never drops it**.
* **[FACT, 1997]** Marky Dubois carries a **live frog in his overalls pocket into the batter's box**.
* **[FACT, 1997]** Dmitri Petrovich's nickname is **"Paste"** and it is earned honestly.
* **[FACT, franchise]** In the credits the kids go to an amusement park, and **Angela Delvecchio
  drops her soda from the top of the Ferris wheel** — the joke keeps running after the game is over.

### 8.3 Rules for writing ours

**[TARGET]** Every game contains at least: **one announcer non sequitur per half-inning**,
**one physical gag per inning**, and **one environmental gag per venue that the player can cause
on purpose**.

1. Call it straight, then be weird. Never both at once.
2. The joke is about food, weather, cowardice, or a thing in the world — never about a kid's body.
3. The straight man never laughs.
4. If a gag can be *caused by the player*, it is worth ten gags that just play.
5. Name things funnily. A great name is a free joke that fires every time it appears.
6. **Period comedy for us:** the ash-can lid used as a base; the ball that goes down a sewer grate
   and the whole game stops while a kid fishes for it; the cop who blows a whistle and everyone
   scatters; the window that *doesn't* break and everyone reacts anyway; the pushcart vendor who
   catches a foul ball in his hat.

## 9. The single-frame test

**The critic's checklist.** These are things that should be true of *almost any* random Backyard
Baseball screenshot, and therefore of almost any random screenshot of ours. **Every one of the 25
is answerable from a single PNG with no motion, no audio and no source access** — if you find a
check here you cannot settle from the image alone, that is a bug in this list, not a judgement
call. Score each **yes/no**, report the count, and name the numbers that failed.
**Below 18/25 is a failing frame.** All measurements assume 1600×900 (§3.6).

| # | Check | Feeds axis |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Big heads.** Every kid's head is 29–33% of standing height, measured crown-to-chin excluding hair (§1.1); shoulders no wider than the head. | Character |
| 2 | **No muscles.** Not one defined muscle on any figure. Limbs are tubes. | Character |
| 3 | **Mitt hands, big shoes.** Hands 55–70% of head width, rounded; shoe length 0.7–0.9 head-heights. | Character |
| 4 | **Six-shape mouths.** Every visible mouth classifies into one of the six shapes in §1.3. | Character |
| 5 | **Nameable silhouettes.** Thresholded to solid black at 96 px tall, ≥ 6 of 9 fielders are still nameable. | Character |
| 6 | **Roster spread.** The head-height ratios in frame span ≥ 1.0 head-height end to end; not everyone is the same build. | Character |
| 7 | **No two adjacent kids share a shirt hue** (< 20° apart in HSL counts as shared). | Readability |
| 8 | **Saturation.** The three largest colour areas are all at S ≥ 0.35. | Craft |
| 9 | **7–12 distinct hues** (≥ 20° apart, ≥ 1% of frame area each). Hard fail below 6 or above 15. | Craft |
| 10 | **Sky is lighter and cooler than the ground** — median L\* difference 15–30 points, sky hue cooler. | Craft |
| 11 | **The upper third is not empty** — cloud, rooftop, wire, bird, laundry, smoke, washing line. | Craft |
| 12 | **Contact shadows under everything** — every kid, base and prop, opacity 0.25–0.45, tinted not grey. | Craft |
| 13 | **The ball is findable in under one second**, at ≥ 4.5:1 contrast against its immediate backdrop. | Readability |
| 14 | **The landing marker / ball shadow is visible** and ≥ 3:1 against this surface, ≥ 14 px across. | Readability |
| 15 | **The pitch-location ring is present and legible** — ≥ 40 px across, ≥ 3:1 contrast (pitch frames only; score N/A elsewhere and drop the denominator to 24). | Readability |
| 16 | **At least three named props** specific to this venue that change how play works, each identifiable without a caption. | Period truth |
| 17 | **The boundary is asymmetric** — different heights, materials and distances around the field. | Period truth |
| 18 | **At least five non-player subjects are caught mid-action** — mid-stride, mid-gesture, mid-flap, mid-pour — none in a symmetric rest pose. | Aliveness |
| 19 | **Spectators are individuals** — 6–15 of them, ≥ 3 distinct silhouettes, none an exact copy of a neighbour. | Aliveness |
| 20 | **On-screen scale is right** — batter/pitcher 18–26% of frame height; no fielder under 8%. | Readability |
| 21 | **Four depth bands are readable**, and one foreground element overlaps the play plane without hiding the ball. | Craft |
| 22 | **HUD ≤ 12% of frame area**, in ≤ 3 corner clusters, inside a 4% safe margin, nothing overlapping the play. | Readability |
| 23 | **The count is legible when the image is downsampled to 480 px wide**, and a base diagram shows occupancy at a glance. | Readability |
| 24 | **Every HUD quantity is a physical object** with a nameable material — stamina especially — and no HUD pixel is a neutral grey with S < 0.10. | Cohesion |
| 25 | **Type is hand-lettered** with per-glyph jitter, sitting on a physical ground (card, plank, chalk patch, taped label), and **a kid's name or nickname is on screen**. No default system font anywhere. | Cohesion |

One more, and it is a veto rather than a point: **no kid is in a symmetrical, neutral, at-rest
pose.** If half the roster is standing to attention, stop scoring and report that.

**Bonus checks for a motion burst.** Capture it with
`node tools/film.mjs <scenario> --frames 12 --step 0.033`, then read the contact sheet in order:

26. Contact produces a **hitstop of 4–6 frames**.
27. The ball **squashes** to 1.35–1.6× on contact and on hard bounces.
28. At least one **secondary element** (hair, hem, cap, ponytail, glove lace) visibly lags the body
    by 2–4 frames.
29. Three or more **distinct, freezable poses** exist inside any single action; no two consecutive
    frames look like a lerp.
30. Within 10 frames of contact, **all runners and the ball are simultaneously visible**.
31. **Anticipation is present** — the frame before any major action moves *away* from it.
32. **No two kids hold the same pose** in the same frame, and ≥ 3 kids change pose across the burst.
33. **The camera moves for information, not for drama** — no push-in, no roll, no shake that is not
    a decaying impact response.

## 10. Translation table — Backyard Baseball → 1920s NYC stickball

The design language is the standard. The props are not. Translate, do not transplant.

| Backyard Baseball (1997) | Our 1920s NYC stickball equivalent |
|---|---|
| Suburban backyard, lawn, picket fence | City block, cobblestone/asphalt, stoops, iron rails |
| The shed in centre field | The fire escape / the corner ash cans / a delivery truck |
| The neighbour's swimming pool | The open cellar door / the sewer grate / the fishmonger's tank |
| Chain-link outfield fence | Tenement wall, brick, painted-over ghost signage |
| Bases: a pizza box (home), a frisbee (1st), a sewer cover (2nd), a sweater (3rd) | Four *different* pieces of junk, never four of the same: an ash-can lid, a chalked square, a flattened cap, a manhole cover |
| Aluminium bat | Broomstick, sawn-off mop handle, a barrel stave |
| Hardball | Pink rubber ball — a fuzzless tennis core, and it *bounces*. Period note: Spalding was selling unfuzzed pink cores as a "High-Bounce Ball" from the 1920s, but the **name "Spaldeen" is a 1940s–50s Brooklyn coinage** — the object is period-true, the word is not, so it must never appear as on-screen text or in an announcer line. |
| Backyard swing set / jungle gym | Hydrant, lamppost, hand-cart, newsstand, pushcart |
| Parents on lawn chairs | Women leaning out third-floor windows on cushions |
| Family dog | Stray dog, pigeons, a cart horse |
| Ice-cream truck jingle | Hurdy-gurdy, a radio through an open window, a knife-grinder's bell |
| Super Colossal Dome (the ladder's top) | A real ballpark visible in the distance — the aspiration |
| Kids' 1990s tees and jeans | Newsboy caps, knickerbockers, suspenders, rolled sleeves, bare feet |
| Vinnie the Gooch (Brooklyn wiseguy) | **Keep him.** He is already period-correct. |
| Rhett Mathis's per-character themes | Klezmer clarinet, ragtime piano, mandolin, cornet, jaw harp |
| Hanna-Barbera cartoon SFX library | **Keep the vocabulary.** Boings and slide whistles are timeless |

**[TARGET]** Period truth and Backyard-ness are not in tension. Anything that reads as generic
"old city" — sepia, desaturation, grime, fog, brown-grey everything — is a *failure of both*.
1920s New York in daylight was loud, crowded, painted and advertised, and the checkable version of
that sentence is: **every street-level frame carries ≥ 3 pieces of hand-lettered signage large
enough to read the words, ≥ 2 striped or coloured awnings, and ≥ 1 painted advertisement on brick**;
the three largest colour areas still clear **S ≥ 0.35** (§2.1); and **no global colour grade,
sepia tint or saturation reduction is applied anywhere in the pipeline**. A period photograph is
monochrome because of the film stock, not because of the street.

## 11. Anti-patterns — the specific ways this gets failed

Every one of these has been observed in unfinished work — items 16 and 17 were observed in *this
document* before it was fact-checked. A critic who spots one should name it by number.

1. **The grey box world.** Correct geometry, zero saturation, no props. Reads as a physics test.
2. **Adult proportions in small hats.** Shoulders wider than heads. Instantly not a kid game.
3. **The recolour roster.** One mesh, nine shirt colours. Fails the blackout test at once.
4. **Floating kids.** No contact shadows. The single most common Craft failure.
5. **The invisible ball.** Correctly simulated, impossible to see. Fails Readability alone.
6. **The empty sky.** Flat gradient, nothing in the upper third.
7. **The dead crowd.** A texture, a billboard, or nine copies of one silhouette in one pose.
8. **The productivity HUD.** Inter/Helvetica, rounded grey rects, thin strokes, a progress bar.
   Diagnostic: point at a HUD element and name the material it is made of. If you can't, it's this.
9. **Lerp animation.** No held poses, no anticipation, no follow-through, no hitstop.
10. **Silence.** No chatter, no ambience, no announcer, no reason to keep watching.
11. **The stapled modules.** Kids in one style, environment in another, UI in a third.
12. **Cute without funny.** Nothing on screen has a setup and a beat. Nobody smiles.
13. **Prestige lighting.** Bloom, vignette, colour grading, shallow depth of field. Wrong genre.
14. **Sepia nostalgia.** Confusing "1920s" with "photographs of the 1920s". The people living in
    1920 did not see their world in brown.
15. **The generic announcer.** Correct calls, no personality, no non sequiturs, ten total lines.
16. **The unmeasurable frame.** Kids rendered so small (or the camera so far out) that head ratios,
    mouth shapes and brow positions cannot be measured at all — §3.6 exists to prevent this, and a
    frame that defeats §9 by being illegible has not passed §9, it has dodged it.
17. **The cross-game pastiche.** Copying a 2001 or 2026 feature and calling it "Backyard Baseball"
    without noticing it did not exist in 1997. Cite the year or don't cite it.

## 12. How to use this as a critic

You are scoring pixels, not prose. Work in this order and do not skip steps.

**Step 1 — Shoot before you read.**
Run `node tools/shoot.mjs --out shots/<your-run-id>` (defaults to 1600×900, one PNG per registered
scenario, plus `report.json`). A non-zero exit, any entry in `report.json`'s `errors`, or any
console warning is an immediate fail; stop and report it. Do not read the builder's summary until
you have formed an opinion from the images — and if a summary is in your prompt, treat it as a
claim to be disproved.

**Step 2 — Run the single-frame test (§9) on at least three frames.**
Pick the frames that should be strongest: the pitch, the contact, and one fielding frame. Score
each out of 25 and **write the numbers down**. Below 18 is a failing frame. Report which specific
numbered checks failed — "fails 5, 11, 16, 18" is actionable; "feels unpolished" is not.

**Step 3 — Do the blackout test yourself, for real.**
Take one fielding frame, threshold it so every kid is solid black on white, scale the kids to about
96 px tall, and try to name them. Write down the count out of nine. Below 6, §1.2 has failed and
Character cannot score above 4 regardless of how nice the textures are. Do not do this "mentally" —
the whole point of the test is that your memory of the frame is more generous than the frame.

**Step 4 — Measure, don't vibe.**
The TARGET numbers exist so you can be specific. Head-to-body ratio. Hue count. HUD area. Shadow
opacity. Downsample to 480px and see if you can still read the count. A critique that contains
numbers cannot be argued away; one that contains adjectives can.

**Step 5 — If the piece is about motion, capture a burst with the tool, not by hand.**
`node tools/film.mjs <scenario> --frames 12 --step 0.033 --out shots/<your-run-id>` renders a
**numbered contact sheet** — twelve frames 1/30 s apart, laid out left-to-right with timestamps —
which is the format in which lerp, missing hitstop and absent anticipation are obvious at a glance.
(It drives `__SB.scenario` / `__SB.advance` / `__SB.renderOnce` for you; do not re-implement it.)
Read the sheet in order against §5's beat table and bonus checks 26–33. Motion that reads fine in a
single frame is routinely dead in sequence — this step is the one that catches it.
